ABSTRACT
Fewer latent tokens promise cheaper diffusion. Yet aggressive spatial compres-
sion creates a reconstruction-generation dilemma: adding channels improves re-
construction but makes generation harder. Our goal is to make extreme spatial
compression competitive by shifting this frontier. We introduce APEX, short for
Asynchronous Prefix denoising for EXtreme compression, which pairs a hierar-
chical latent space with asynchronous denoising over channel groups. Our autoen-
coder orders channel prefixes from structure to detail, while one shared denoiser
generates them at staggered local times without adding tokens. We show that
naively combining a channel hierarchy with asynchronous denoising can make
generation worse. APEX succeeds by matching what each level represents, when
it denoises, and how training is allocated across the trajectory. On ImageNet-512,
APEX outperforms existing methods at every evaluated channel count under 64×
and 128× spatial compression, substantially improving this frontier. At matched
training and guided sampling compute, it also outperforms a 256-token model at
every evaluated budget, with larger gains at lower compute. APEX makes ex-
treme spatial compression practical for compute-efficient latent diffusion. Code is
available at https://daviddinkevich.github.io/APEX.

INTRODUCTION
Modern diffusion models increasingly use Transformers, whose attention cost grows quadratically
with the number of input tokens. Latent diffusion reduces this cost by compressing an image into
a smaller spatial grid and applying diffusion there (Rombach et al., 2022). Our goal is to make ex-
treme spatial compression competitive for latent diffusion. Stronger spatial compression produces
fewer tokens. Channel count is a separate design choice, but preserving reconstruction quality typ-
ically requires adding channels as the grid shrinks, producing increasingly wide latents. This trade
is attractive because shorter sequences can substantially reduce denoiser cost, permitting more opti-
mization and sampling under a fixed compute budget. This suggests an obvious strategy: compress
the spatial grid as aggressively as possible, then add enough channels to preserve reconstruction
quality.
Unfortunately, this strategy encounters a well-established reconstruction-generation dilemma (Yao
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025c;d; Lai et al., 2025). At fixed spatial compression, adding channels
improves reconstruction but makes the resulting latent harder to generate. Additional channels let
the encoder preserve more image-specific information, including exact textures and local details.
This lowers reconstruction error, but increases the information that the diffusion model must pro-
duce (Chen et al., 2025d; Heek et al., 2026). Standard latent diffusion therefore faces a poor choice:
use fewer channels and sacrifice reconstruction quality, or preserve more detail and sacrifice gener-
ation quality. Existing approaches reduce this tension by restricting latent information or importing
pretrained semantic structure (Kingma & Welling, 2013; Yu et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Zheng
et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025). The closest structured-latent method in our setting organizes channel
prefixes within one reconstruction latent, but still denoises each retained prefix at a shared timestep
(Chen et al., 2025d). This limitation becomes especially severe at 128× spatial compression, where
a 5122 image is represented by a 4 × 4 grid of only 16 tokens.
APEX addresses this problem without imposing an additional bottleneck on the final latent. Instead,
it changes how the channels are organized and generated. Coarse structure must be generated from

We therefore factorize generation into structure generation followed by conditional refinement. Our
autoencoder orders channel prefixes from structure to detail by supervising them at progressively
higher resolutions. During diffusion, one shared Transformer denoises the newly added channel
groups at staggered local times, so each refinement is predicted from cleaner preceding context.
As this is done along the channel dimension, APEX does not increase the number of tokens. Fig-
ure 1 summarizes the representation and denoising process. Although hierarchical channel latents
and asynchronous denoising each exist, combining them is not automatic: shared-time diffusion
performs poorly on APEX levels, while staggered local times perform poorly with the DC-AE 1.5
hierarchy and prefix sampler (Table 2).
We evaluate APEX on ImageNet-512 at 64× and 128× spatial compression. We write f64c128
for 64× spatial compression with 128 channels. At 5122, f64 and f128 correspond to 64 and
16 tokens. Across every evaluated channel count, APEX outperforms all compared methods and
degrades far less as channels are added. At f128c512, APEX reaches 8.98 guided gFID from 16
tokens with SiT-XL, while every compared method remains in the double digits. Our 64-token
f64c128 model matches the gFID of the widely used 1024-token f8c4 SD-VAE (Rombach et al.,
2022) using 15% of its denoiser-training FLOPs (Appendix B.4). With training compute matched,
our 64-token hierarchical model also outperforms a less-compressed 256-token model across every
evaluated guided inference budget, with larger gains as sampling compute decreases. These results
show that once the reconstruction-generation dilemma is addressed, extreme spatial compression
becomes a quality-compute advantage rather than only a per-step speedup.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. Making hierarchy and asynchronous denoising work together. We show that nested
channel levels and staggered local times do not combine automatically. APEX couples
resolution-supervised levels, local times, and training allocation within one fixed-token
latent.
2. Turning high compression into a compute advantage. With training compute matched,
APEX at 64 tokens outperforms a less-compressed 256-token baseline across every evalu-
ated guided inference budget, with larger gains at lower compute.
3. Generating without an additional information bottleneck. Rather than improving gen-
erative tractability by restricting what the latent can store, APEX improves generative
tractability while maintaining comparable reconstruction quality, without imposing an ad-
ditional explicit information bottleneck.

RELATED WORK
Deep Compression Autoencoders.
Deep-compression autoencoders reduce diffusion sequence
length by moving representational capacity from spatial positions into latent channels. DC-AE

made reconstruction practical at 32×, 64×, and 128× spatial compression and provides our base
architecture (Chen et al., 2025c). DC-AE 1.5 structures channel prefixes and trains diffusion on ran-
domly sampled prefixes at a shared timestep, while FreqWarm modifies diffusion training through a
frequency curriculum (Chen et al., 2025d; Lai et al., 2025). These are our closest direct baselines.
APEX instead pairs resolution-matched cumulative levels with staggered local times; APEX levels
under shared-time diffusion and a direct application of the staggered schedule to the DC-AE 1.5
construction perform poorly (Table 2).
Latent Information and Semantic Alignment.
Existing methods improve diffusibility either by
controlling latent information or importing pretrained semantic structure. Traditional VAEs im-
pose a Gaussian prior, Unified Latents explicitly controls information rate with a learned diffusion
prior, and MAETok uses a non-variational discriminative latent for 128-token generation (Kingma
& Welling, 2013; Heek et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025b). Semantic-alignment methods instead shape
the latent or denoiser using pretrained representations (Yu et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Zheng et al.,
2025; Kouzelis et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025; Leng et al., 2025). SFD and Learning When to De-
noise additionally pair a DINOv2-derived semantic latent with a separate texture latent and denoise
semantics first using a fixed or learned relative schedule (Pan et al., 2025; Qian & Cheng, 2026;
Oquab et al., 2024). APEX uses no external semantic representation or explicit latent prior. It in-
duces structure within one reconstruction latent, leaving later levels free to preserve image-specific
detail.
Hierarchical and Frequency-Based Representations.
Coarse-to-fine representations appear in
Matryoshka learning, cascaded and nested diffusion, multiscale tokenizers, ordered-channel mod-
els, and flexible-length token sequences (Kusupati et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2024;
Chen et al., 2025a; Zhong et al., 2025; Paul et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026; Bachmann et al., 2025).
Frequency-based methods instead reshape latent spectra or explicitly separate coarse and fine com-
ponents (Ning et al., 2026; Skorokhodov et al., 2025; Kouzelis et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025; Guan
et al., 2026). Denoising-VAE constructs progressively low-pass latent levels with blurred image
targets and frequency-aligned diffusion (Xiang et al., 2025); we use Spectrum Matching as the
representative autoencoder-side baseline for this family. WaiT similarly delays wavelet-detail gen-
eration, but operates in pixel space rather than a learned high-compression latent (Pavasovic et al.,
2026). APEX instead uses cumulative channel prefixes of one jointly decoded latent on a fixed
spatial grid, supervising each prefix at a corresponding resolution and generating later groups from
cleaner preceding levels.

METHOD
APEX has two components. First, a single autoencoder orders cumulative channel levels from struc-
ture to detail using progressively higher-resolution targets (Section 3.1). Second, a shared Trans-
former denoises the channels introduced at each level at staggered local times, allowing later levels
to refine cleaner earlier ones without adding spatial tokens (Section 3.2; Figure 2). Pseudocode
for autoencoder training, diffusion training, and inference is given in Appendices C and D (Algo-
rithms 1, 2, and 3).

DiT initialization.
We initialize the rows of the input projection associated with b0 using the
standard SiT initialization (Ma et al., 2024) and the rows associated with b1, . . . , bL−1 to zero.
The model therefore begins as an L0-only generator and gradually learns to incorporate refinement
information, without modifying the Transformer trunk.

EXPERIMENTS
We target the operating point where each token must absorb substantial instance-specific detail: high
native resolution under aggressive spatial compression. Following DC-AE (Chen et al., 2025c), we
evaluate on ImageNet-512 (Deng et al., 2009) at 64× and 128× spatial compression. We first com-
pare reconstruction and generation across f64 and f128 latents with increasing channel capacity, then
test whether high compression remains advantageous when both training and inference are compute-
constrained. We next perform an extensive component study showing that the gain emerges from
the complete representation–diffusion co-design: neither the hierarchical latent nor asynchronous
denoising reaches the same quality on its own. Finally, we study the schedule choices used by the
main models.
Setup.
We denote an autoencoder with f× spatial compression and C channels by ffcC. At
5122, f32, f64, and f128 produce 16 × 16, 8 × 8, and 4 × 4 latent grids, corresponding to 256,
64, and 16 Transformer tokens. APEX uses c8−32−128 at f64 and c32−128−256−512 at f128;
wider variants append c256 and c512 at f64 and c1024 at f128. Appendix Table 5 gives the exact
groups and target resolutions. Unless stated otherwise, we use SiT-XL/1 (Ma et al., 2024), with
SiT-B/1 for component and design ablations. We report rFID on the ImageNet validation set and
gFID from 50K class-conditional samples using the ADM protocol (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021).
Generation uses Euler sampling and CFG (Ho & Salimans, 2022) with scale 1.5 unless otherwise
specified; Appendix E additionally reports unguided gFID, sFID, and Inception Score. Within each

Baselines.
DC-AE uses the unstructured reconstruction latent of Chen et al. (2025c). DC-AE 1.5
trains cumulative channel prefixes and augments diffusion with randomly sampled prefixes under a
shared timestep (Chen et al., 2025d). FreqWarm changes the frequency curriculum of the diffusion
model without modifying the autoencoder (Lai et al., 2025). Spectrum Matching regularizes the
latent and reconstruction spectra (Ning et al., 2026). We implement all baselines in the same DC-
AE and SiT training framework.

5
CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
We introduced APEX, which pairs a resolution-aware hierarchy of channel prefixes with asyn-
chronous denoising across channels. At 64× and 128× spatial compression, APEX substantially
shifts the reconstruction–generation frontier as channels are added. With matched training com-
pute, it also outperforms a less-compressed 256-token baseline across all evaluated guided inference
budgets, making extreme compression competitive under constrained compute.
APEX still requires choosing channel boundaries, per-level target resolutions, the stagger gap, and
the global-clock distribution. Section 4.4 provides practical guidance, but the optimal choices may
vary across settings. Learning them jointly with the autoencoder and denoiser is a promising direc-
tion.

We train on the ImageNet-1K training split at 512 × 512 (Deng et al., 2009). Main experiments use

Baselines.
All methods use the same DC-AE and SiT training framework. DC-AE uses an un-
structured latent (Chen et al., 2025c); DC-AE 1.5 uses cumulative prefixes and shared-timestep
prefix training (Chen et al., 2025d). FreqWarm applies its frequency curriculum to diffusion training

B.3
SAMPLING AND EVALUATION
We evaluate generation using 50,000 class-conditional samples and the ADM protocol (Dhariwal &
Nichol, 2021), reporting gFID (Heusel et al., 2017), sFID (Nash et al., 2021), and Inception Score
(IS) (Salimans et al., 2016). We use classifier-free guidance with scale 1.5 unless another scale is
specified (Ho & Salimans, 2022). Unguided sampling retains the class label but does not apply
guidance (CFG 1.0). We report unguided gFID for the channel-scaling comparisons wherever the
guided checkpoint is identified, and for the SiT-B gap and clock-allocation ablations, using the same
trained model and number of sampling steps as the guided evaluation. Reconstruction rFID and
PSNR are evaluated on the ImageNet validation split using the complete latent, following DC-AE
(Chen et al., 2025c).
For N sampling steps, denoiser inference cost is NFforward without guidance and 2NFforward with
guidance, excluding the autoencoder decoder. One f32 evaluation costs approximately four times
an f64 evaluation. The 1× budget in Figure 6 therefore pairs 64 f32 evaluations with 250 f64
evaluations. APEX sampling follows the Euler update in Section D; sampling budgets are given
with the individual comparisons.

D.1
THE STAGGERED CLOCK
The global clock runs from τ = 0 to T = 1 + (L −1)g, where L is the number of levels and g is
the stagger gap. Each level has a local diffusion time
tℓ(τ) = clip(1 + ℓ· g −τ, 0, 1).
(11)
Local time decreases from 1 (noise) to 0 (clean). Level ℓstarts at τ = ℓ· g (a multiple of the gap)
and finishes at τ = 1 + ℓ· g. Earlier levels are therefore cleaner than the later levels they condition.

D.3
DIFFUSION TRAINING
Unstarted groups are zero, active groups are mixed with Gaussian noise, and completed groups
provide clean context. The target velocity is noise minus the clean latent. We average the squared
error within each active group and then equally across active groups. Unstarted and completed
groups do not contribute to the loss.
The input-projection weights for L0 use the standard SiT initialization (Ma et al., 2024); those for
later groups are initialized to zero. The Transformer is conditioned on the global clock.

F.1
FIGURE 1: OVERVIEW
Purpose.
Show how APEX organizes a wide latent, denoises its levels asynchronously, and
changes the reconstruction–generation trade-off.
Procedure.
Panel (a) is a schematic of an f128c512 latent: a 5122 image is encoded onto a 4 × 4
grid of 16 tokens, and the 512 channels of each token are ordered from structure to detail. Panel
(b) shows the corresponding staggered schedule on one global clock τ: each later level starts after
a delay g, so it is denoised while earlier levels are already cleaner. Panel (c) is a schematic of the
reconstruction–generation frontier relative to a flat latent; the points and curves are illustrative, not
measured. The quantitative five-method comparison at f128c512 is Figure 5.
F.2
FIGURE 2: AUTOENCODER, TRAINING, AND INFERENCE
Purpose.
Show how APEX changes autoencoder training, diffusion training, and sampling.
Procedure.
The autoencoder panel encodes an image, samples a cumulative prefix, zeros the re-
maining channels, and decodes at full resolution. Reconstruction and target are then resized to the
prefix’s target resolution before the loss. The example retains the first two of four levels. The training
panel samples a global clock from q(τ) and maps it to local times with Equation 11. With g = 0.25
and τ = 0.38, the first two local times are 0.62 and 0.87; the third level has not started. Active
groups are noised at these times, the unstarted group is zeroed, and one shared Transformer predicts
their velocities. The inference panel shows the same three-level clock at the activation points: at
τ = 0 only L0 is active; at τ = g and τ = 2g, L1 and L2 are initialized from Gaussian noise.
Generation continues until every group is clean.

Purpose.
Show how training examples are allocated along the global clock.
Procedure.
