## Abstract 

Weight-space networks operate directly on parameters of other neural networks, enabling tasks such as predicting model properties, editing trained models, and generating weights. Equivariance has emerged as a key design principle for these networks, owing to weight-space symmetries such as neuron permutations. How-ever, existing equivariant weight-space architectures have primarily been studied for transformations that preserve the network architecture. In contrast, many prac-tical transformations, including model compression and upscaling, map a trained source network into a target network with a different architecture. In this setting, the source and target permutation symmetries act on different parameter spaces, making equivariance less straightforward to formulate. Our key idea for address-ing this mismatch is to reformulate cross-architecture operators with two inputs: a trained source network and an initialization of the target network. This lets us define equivariant cross-architecture operators that refine the initialization of the target network using information from the source network, while being invariant to source-network permutations and equivariant to target-network permutations. Based on this formulation, we introduce CrossGMN, a graph metanetwork that jointly processes both networks through symmetry-preserving cross-network mes-sage passing. We prove CrossGMN is universal for continuous cross-architecture operators on compact sets under a general-position assumption. We further eval-uate CrossGMN for model compression, where it predicts the parameters of a smaller network to accelerate subsequent knowledge distillation. Across 2-D and 3-D INRs and image classification with MLPs, CNNs, and Vision Transformers, CrossGMN speeds up distillation by up to 8.89×, transfers across datasets without retraining (3.78×), and a single model can accelerate compression from heteroge-neous source architectures into a common target architecture. 

## 1 Introduction 

Weight-space learning studies neural architectures that operate directly on the parameters of other neural networks, enabling tasks such as predicting model properties, editing trained models, and gen-erating weights (Unterthiner et al., 2020; Eilertsen et al., 2020; Sch¨ urholt et al., 2021; 2022a; 2024). A central property of weight spaces is their permutation symmetries, resulting in many different model parameterizations that represent the same function. This has motivated the development of permutation-equivariant weight-space architectures (Navon et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023a;b; 2024; Lim et al., 2024; Kofinas et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2024; 2025; Vo et al., 2025), which represent the current state of the art for learning over weights in many tasks. Many practical transformations of neural network weights do not preserve the network architec-ture. Model compression maps a trained network into a smaller one for deployment under memory, latency, or compute constraints, through structured pruning (Li et al., 2017) or knowledge distil-lation (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021). Cross-architecture knowledge transfer moves the behavior of a trained network into a structurally different one (Ahn et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024; Czyzewski, 2021). Model upscaling initializes a wider or deeper network from a trained smaller one, allowing training to continue from the transferred parameters rather than from scratch (Chen et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Pham et al., 2024). All of these map the weights of one architecture into the parameter space of another, and most of them are real-ized today by a separate optimization process run from scratch for every source network. Learning such a transformation instead would replace this per-network optimization with a single feedforward 1arXiv:2610.01649v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 pass: one weight-space model is trained once, and every new source network is then transformed in a fraction of a second. Existing equivariant weight-space architectures, however, have exclusively addressed mappings whose input and output lie in the same weight space. The cross-architecture setting is fundamentally different: the source and target networks carry distinct symmetry groups acting on distinct parameter spaces, so it is not even clear what equivariance should mean. Design-ing equivariant cross-architecture weight-space models is thus a central yet overlooked challenge in weight-space learning. Our key observation is that cross-architecture transformations typically come with a natural second input. Whether the target is smaller or larger than the source, the procedure always begins from an initialized target network, which is then optimized against the trained source network, as in knowledge distillation and its variants (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021; Nasser et al., 2024). The transformation is therefore a function of two arguments: the trained source network and an initialization of the target. Importantly, taking both as input resolves the difficulty above. The initialization specifies the target architecture and carries the target symmetry group, so the operator has a well-defined action on each of its arguments: it should be invariant to symmetries of the source network and equivariant to symmetries of the target. This leads us to study equivariant cross-architecture operators F : Vsrc × V tgt → V tgt .

With the right class of operators in place, the remaining question is how to parameterize it. We introduce CrossGMN , a graph metanetwork for learning such operators. CrossGMN represents each of the two networks as a parameter graph, joins them with cross edges, and performs message passing over the resulting joint graph. Information can thus flow between the source and the target while the two permutation symmetries are respected independently. We prove that CrossGMN is a universal approximator of continuous equivariant cross-architecture operators on compact sets under a general-position assumption, and extend this result to finite collections of source architectures mapping into a common target weight space. We evaluate CrossGMN for model compression through knowledge distillation. Given a trained teacher (source network) and an initialization for a smaller student (target network), CrossGMN predicts refined student parameters intended to reduce the number of distillation steps needed to match the teacher. We evaluate width, depth, and combined compressions across 2-D and 3-D INRs (Sitzmann et al., 2020) and MNIST (LeCun et al., 1998)/CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, 2009) classifiers, on architectures including MLPs, CNNs, and Vision Transformers (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021). Across these settings, CrossGMN accelerates subsequent distillation for initializing compressed students, achieving speedups of up to 8.89 × on MNIST INRs and 6.85 × on ModelNet (Wu et al., 2015) INRs. CrossGMN also generalizes out of distribution: a model trained on MNIST INRs transfers to FashionMNIST (Xiao et al., 2017), achieving up to 3.78 × speedup without retraining. More-over, a single CrossGMN can compress heterogeneous CIFAR-10 MLP teacher architectures into a common smaller student architecture and generalize to unseen teacher architectures. In summary, our contributions are: (1) formulating cross-architecture weight-space learning as learn-ing equivariant operators on a source network and a target initialization, which makes the problem’s symmetry structure well-defined; (2) introducing CrossGMN, a graph metanetwork for learning equivariant transformations between different neural-network weight spaces; (3) establishing its universality for continuous cross-architecture operators, including settings with multiple source ar-chitectures; and (4) demonstrating its effectiveness for model compression, where it accelerates knowledge distillation across diverse architectures and out-of-distribution settings.

## 3 Cross-Architecture Weight-Space Transformations 

Let Asrc and Atgt be two architectures with weight spaces Vsrc and Vtgt , and permutation groups

Limitations of predicting the target directly from the source. One could directly map source parameters to target parameters, T : Vsrc −→ V tgt . However, this formulation has two difficulties. First, its equivariance structure is unclear: because the source and target have different permutation groups acting on different weight spaces, T must be invariant to source permutations while selecting a representative among equivalent target parameterizations. Second, natural source-to-target maps may be discontinuous and thus cannot be uniformly approximated arbitrarily well by continuous neural networks. Appendix C.1 gives a simple setting of model compression where mapping each larger network to an optimal smaller one is discontinuous. Instead, we formulate the problem as refinement from an initialization of the target network; this gives rise to more natural continuous cross-architecture operators. This leads to the following defi-nition.

## 4 CrossGMN 

To learn the cross-architecture operators defined in the previous section (Definition 4), we introduce CrossGMN. Section 4.1 presents its architecture, and Section 4.2 establishes its universality for continuous cross-architecture operators. 

4.1 Architecture 

For parameters v, let P(v) = ( V, E ) denote the parameter graph constructed as in GMN (Lim et al., 2024). For an MLP, neurons form the nodes, weights are represented as directed edges between consecutive layers, and biases as node features. We first determine how the neurons of a source layer are joined to those of a target layer, which is dictated by this invariance requirement. We then choose which pairs of layers to join, which is a free design choice. 

Joining two layers. Consider a source hidden layer and a target hidden layer. Since the neurons of each can be permuted independently, the only nonempty set of edges between them that is invariant to both permutations is the complete bipartite one, connecting every source neuron to every target neuron. We call this a layerwise join , following the graph join of two disjoint graphs (Cardoso et al., 2013). Input and output neurons, in contrast, are indexed by the coordinates of the realized function’s input and output, and are not permuted. Since the source and target share input and output dimensions, we join them with the sparser positionwise join : the i-th source input neuron is connected only to the i-th target input neuron, and likewise for output neurons. 

Choosing which layers to join. Because each layerwise join is invariant on its own, any choice of which source and target hidden layers to join yields a symmetry-preserving E×. We call this choice a cross-edge policy . The most permissive policy, all-to-all , joins every source hidden layer to every target hidden layer, giving nm layerwise joins for networks with n and m hidden layers. To avoid this quadratic growth with depth, we use the following strided policy. 

Definition 5 (Strided cross-edge policy) . For two networks with n ≥ m hidden layers, the strided 

policy partitions the n hidden layers of the deeper network into m consecutive blocks of near-equal size, and joins the k-th hidden layer of the shallower network to every layer in the k-th block. Each hidden layer of the deeper network is thus joined to exactly one hidden layer of the shallower network, for a total of n layerwise joins. For example, with n = 10 and m = 5 , the k-th hidden layer of the shallower network is joined to layers 2k − 1 and 2k of the deeper network, giving 10

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate CrossGMN for model compression through knowledge distillation, a practical instance of the cross-architecture operators discussed in Section 3. Following that example, we refer to the source network as the teacher and the target network as the student. We measure whether student ini-tializations predicted by CrossGMN accelerate subsequent distillation toward their teachers. Our ex-periments cover several model families and compression settings, including transfer across datasets and to unseen source architectures. Full experimental details are provided in Appendix D.1–D.7. 

5.1 Experimental setup and evaluation Training and evaluation protocol. For each compression setting, we train CrossGMN on a pop-ulation of trained teachers and student initializations using the distillation objective in equation 3. Given a teacher vT , CrossGMN predicts ˆvS = CrossGMN θ (vT , v0), where v0 is an initializa-tion of the target network as defined in Section 3, which we refer to as the anchor throughout the compression experiments. We initialize the anchor v0 by applying structured pruning methods to the teacher vT , as this pro-vides a stronger starting point for knowledge distillation than random initialization with little addi-tional compression overhead (Xu et al., 2024). For each experiment, we select the structured-pruning method that yields the lowest loss at initialization among the evaluated methods, providing a strong anchor for CrossGMN to improve upon. We compare CrossGMN with the selected anchor itself and with other structured-pruning baselines, including magnitude-based (Xu et al., 2024), activation-based (Hu et al., 2016), and OBS/OBC pruning (Hassibi & Stork, 1992; Frantar & Alistarh, 2022). Appendix D.2 details the anchor and baseline initialization methods. At evaluation, we measure the distillation speedup of CrossGMN relative to the anchor separately for each held-out teacher. ## 6 Related Work 

Weight-space learning treats neural-network parameters as structured data (Han et al., 2026). The pioneering works that started exploring this direction are Eilertsen et al. (2020); Unterthiner et al. (2020); Sch¨ urholt et al. (2021). Later approaches use symmetry considerations and include equiv-ariant weight-space networks (Navon et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023a;b; Tran et al., 2025) and graph-based models (Lim et al., 2024; Kofinas et al., 2024), with extensions to additional sym-metries (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2024). Importantly, these symmetry-aware archi-tectures operate within a single weight space, whereas we study transformations between distinct source and target weight spaces. Related parameter-generation methods either condition on a target architecture (Zhang et al., 2019; Knyazev et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019) or learn distributions over trained-model populations (Sch¨ urholt et al., 2022a; 2024; Erkoc ¸ et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Soro et al., 2025). They generate new weights rather than transforming a particular trained network into another weight space. An extended discussion is provided in Appendix A. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We explored learning transformations between the weight spaces of different neural architectures and introduced CrossGMN, an equivariant framework for learning such cross-architecture operators. CrossGMN is a graph metanetwork that operates jointly over the source and target weight spaces, using a trained source network to refine an initialization of the target network. We proved universal-ity for continuous cross-architecture operators and evaluated the framework on model compression with knowledge distillation. Across INRs and classifiers, CrossGMN reduces subsequent distilla-tion steps for both width and depth compression, while supporting reuse across source architectures, transfer across datasets, and partial generalization to unseen source architectures. These results show that equivariant models can learn architecture-changing transformations directly in weight space. 

Limitations and future work. Our current setting requires access to source parameters, assumes compatible input and output spaces, and is evaluated only at moderate scale (up to roughly 200 k pa-rameters). Scaling to larger models may require sparse or blockwise joint graphs and more efficient cross-network communication. Future work could also extend the operator view beyond compres-sion to settings such as model upscaling and cross-family transfer. 9Acknowledgments

15 A Extended Related Work 

Weight-space learning. Weight-space learning treats neural-network parameters as structured data for learning, representation, and manipulation (Han et al., 2026). DWSNets (Navon et al., 2023) and Neural Functional Networks (Zhou et al., 2023a) construct permutation-equivariant layers di-rectly in weight space, while Neural Functional Transformers (Zhou et al., 2023b) use attention. Graph Metanetworks (Lim et al., 2024) and Neural Graph models (Kofinas et al., 2024) instead rep-resent networks as graphs and apply message passing. Related work also considers additional sym-metries (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2024), structured parameterizations such as LoRA weights (Yang et al., 2026), and related neural artifacts such as gradients (Gelberg et al., 2025) and activations (Bar-Shalom et al., 2025). Universal Neural Functionals extend equivariant weight-space models to general architectures (Zhou et al., 2024), while related approaches extend neural function-als to Transformers and polynomial weight-space features (Tran et al., 2025; Vo et al., 2025). These models have also been used to learn operators on trained networks, including optimization (Kofinas et al., 2024) and pruning-oriented editing (Liu et al., 2025). Existing expressivity results compare such architectures over a single weight space (Dayan et al., 2026). In contrast, CrossGMN studies learned operators between distinct source and target weight spaces with independent permutation actions. 

Parameter generation. Parameter-generation methods amortize weight generation across archi-tectures, tasks, or model populations. Graph HyperNetworks (Zhang et al., 2019; Knyazev et al., 2021) and MetaPruning (Liu et al., 2019) take a target architecture as input and predict its weights. Hyper-representations (Sch¨ urholt et al., 2022a; 2024) and diffusion-based generators (Erkoc ¸ et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Soro et al., 2025) instead learn a distribution over a population of trained models and sample new weights, optionally conditioned on the task or dataset. These methods gen-erate weights for an architecture or population rather than transforming a particular trained source network from one weight space into another. 

Source-conditioned architecture changes. Net2Net provides analytic function-preserving trans-formations that widen or deepen a network (Chen et al., 2016), and model-growth methods extend this approach to pretrained Transformers (Chen et al., 2022). LiGO (Wang et al., 2023) learns a structured linear map from a smaller pretrained model to an initialization of a larger one. While these methods use analytic growth rules or optimize a growth map for a given pretrained source model, CrossGMN learns a shared operator across a population of source networks and reuses it on unseen sources without fitting a new operator for each model. 

Compression and distillation. Structured pruning constructs a smaller network by removing en-tire units or channels and reusing the retained weights (Li et al., 2017). Knowledge distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s outputs (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021). FitNets addi-tionally uses intermediate teacher representations as hints to train thinner, deeper students (Romero et al., 2015). Distillation can also start from weights selected from the teacher (Xu et al., 2024). CrossGMN fits between these stages: it jointly processes the teacher and the structured-pruning ini-tialization (the anchor) to predict a refined student initialization, from which distillation proceeds toward the same teacher. The refinement is learned across a population of teachers to accelerate this subsequent optimization.

Definition 7 (Parameter graph) . For parameters v ∈ V A, the parameter graph PA(v) = ( V, E )

is a typed directed multigraph. Unit nodes represent neurons, channels, or attention coordinates. Auxiliary nodes represent parameter vectors not indexed by two units, such as biases and the gain and shift parameters of normalization layers. Every learnable scalar is stored on either a node or an edge: a matrix entry [W ]ij is carried by the edge j → i; the entries of a convolutional kernel give parallel edges between its input and output channels; and bias or normalization entries are stored on their unit node or on an edge from the corresponding auxiliary node. A fixed parameter-to-graph encoding specifies this assignment and the correspondence used to decode graph outputs into network parameters (Appendix D.1). Nodes carry layer and source/target labels; classifier nodes also carry node type, canonical input/output indices, and optional architecture-family and activation-type labels. Edges carry their scalar value, layer index, and role (source-internal, target-internal, source-to-target, or target-to-source in the joint graph), with an optional architecture-family label for classifiers. Following Lim et al. (2024, Section 2.2 and Appendix C.2), a neural DAG automorphism preserves edge incidence, fixes input, output, and bias nodes, and preserves weight-sharing constraints. We use the term neural graph automorphism for the corresponding relabelings of our parameter and joint graphs, which may include reciprocal edges. We require these relabelings to fix auxiliary nodes and preserve all structural node and edge features, as in Lim et al. (2024, Appendix C.6). Parameter values are permuted with their nodes or edges, rather than held numerically fixed. Message passing with shared functions is equivariant to these relabelings. The layerwise joins of Section 4.1 are pre-served under independent relabelings of their two endpoint layers, while positionwise joins connect only canonically indexed boundary nodes. Hence the joint graph preserves the source and target actions independently. For an MLP, the automorphisms of the standard parameter graph are precisely the hidden-neuron permutations in Definition 2 (Lim et al., 2024, Proposition 5). We first specify the shared cross-edge policy, then give the full definitions for MLPs, CNNs, and Transformers.

B.2.5 Equivariance of the joint graph Proposition 2 (Equivariance induced by the joint graph) . Let J be the joint graph of Equation 4, with the cross-edge construction of Section 4.1 (and Appendix B.2.2) for MLPs, Appendix B.2.3 for CNNs, or Appendix B.2.4 for Transformers. Let GMN θ be a graph metanetwork as defined by Lim et al. (2024, Section 2.3), and let Hsrc and Htgt be parameter-permutation groups induced by neural graph automorphisms of the respective parameter graphs. Then the CrossGMN operator of Definition 6 satisfies, for every choice of parameters θ,

CrossGMN θ (gsrc · vsrc , g tgt · vinit ) = gtgt · CrossGMN θ (vsrc , vinit )

for all gsrc ∈ Hsrc and gtgt ∈ Htgt and all source and target parameters. For MLPs, we may take 

Hsrc = Gsrc and Htgt = Gtgt , so this is exactly the symmetry required by Definition 4. Proof. Fix (gsrc , g tgt ) ∈ Hsrc × Htgt and write J = J (vsrc , vinit ). Let γ be the disjoint union of inducing automorphisms of the two parameter graphs. Each layerwise join is preserved because its endpoint sets are preserved; any positionwise boundary edges are fixed. Cross-edge features are 22 unchanged within each directed join. Thus γ preserves the joint graph’s structure and structural features, and 

J (gsrc · vsrc , g tgt · vinit ) = γ · J .

Graph metanetworks commute with such relabelings (Lim et al., 2024, Appendix C.6), so GMN θ (γ·J ) = γ · GMN θ (J ). Moreover, the target projection satisfies Πtgt (γ · Z) = gtgt · Πtgt (Z) for every joint output Z: it discards source entries and decodes target entries into their corresponding parameter coordinates. Consequently, 

∆θ (gsrc · vsrc , g tgt · vinit ) = Π tgt (GMN θ (γ · J )) = Π tgt (γ · GMN θ (J )) = gtgt · ∆θ (vsrc , vinit ).

Since gtgt acts linearly on target parameters, adding the transformed initialization and applying the fixed residual scale s in Equation 5 gives the claimed identity. Taking gtgt = e gives source invariance, and taking gsrc = e gives target equivariance.

D.1 CrossGMN implementation 

All experiments use the joint graph of the teacher vT and anchor v0. An equivariant graph metanet-work processes this graph, and the target-side readout predicts the residual in Equation 5. In the classifier experiments, node features concatenate a scalar, an 8-dimensional layer embedding, a 4-dimensional source/target-role embedding, a 4-dimensional node-type embedding, and optional 

4-dimensional family and activation embeddings. Edge features concatenate the parameter value, the layer embedding, a 4-dimensional edge-role embedding, and an optional family embedding. In the INR experiments, node and edge inputs both have dimension 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 : scalar value, layer embedding, and role embedding. Parameter values are not normalized. The four edge roles are source-internal, target-internal, source-to-target, and target-to-source. 

Cross edges. Cross edges use the layerwise joins and matching notation of Appendix B.2.1. The formal joint graphs in Appendices B.2.2–B.2.4 use positionwise input/output joins. The CNN and ViT classifier encoding uses dense boundary joins, replacing Pos by Join in Equation 7; MLP boundaries remain positionwise. Both choices preserve the stated symmetries because input and output nodes are fixed. 

Backbone and readout. We use a bidirectional S CALE GMN backbone (Kalogeropoulos et al., 2024), motivated by its better empirical performance than plain GMN in most experiments. Our universality results are established for plain GMN. The backbone uses skip connections, edge updates, and dropout 0.1 during operator training. Message and update functions are two-layer SiLU MLPs with layer normalization. D.11 Classifier teacher-population diversity 

As noted in Section 5.1, amortizing a learned compression operator across a model population is motivated by the population spanning diverse functions. We therefore measure diversity in classifier teachers’ outputs, which directly characterize the functions that their students must preserve. Param-eter differences alone would be insufficient, since different weights can realize the same function. 

Populations and sampling. Table 18 reports averages over 10 ,000 randomly sampled pairs of distinct teachers from each classifier population, using the full held-out cohort evaluated for com-pression. Settings that share a teacher population share its diversity statistics.
