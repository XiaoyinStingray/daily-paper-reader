# Lossy Compressive Text Autoencoders 

Vinko Sabolčec , Angelos Katharopoulos , David Grangier 

Apple Our work explores learning a compressed latent representation of text, at the intersection of data compression and repre-sentation learning. We propose an autoencoder architecture that performs residual downscaling and upscaling of hidden representations along the time axis, with a residual low-dimension discrete bottleneck. We analyze our approach for differ-ent quantization methods, training objectives, and datasets. For different levels of compression, we evaluate the similarity between the original and reconstructed text both at the surface-level (BLEU) and at the semantic-level (LLM-based judge). Additionally, we evaluate our models on downstream question-answering and semantic text similarity benchmarks. Our approach results in compressed representations which are on par with lossless text compression algorithms at 2.24 bits per byte on web text data, while having good reconstruction and downstream task performance.

# 1 Introduction 

The ability to represent information efficiently is at the heart of both data compression and artificial intelli-gence. Whether the goal is compression per se or generating the next-token prediction using a model trained on a large corpus, the underlying challenge is the same: learning to represent data in a way that preserves its essential meaning in the smallest possible space (Delétang et al., 2024). While the current language models (LMs) have demonstrated the ability to learn highly useful representations, these representations are often massive in scale compared to the raw information they represent. Furthermore, compression is usually treated as a separate utility which remains disconnected from the structure of the representation space. In this work, we explore the joint goal of representation learning and data compression using neural models. While there is existing work focusing on compression, such as lossless text compressors using entropy coding on the next-token probability distribution (Bellard, 2024; Tacconelli, 2026) and context compression for faster inference (Zaheer et al., 2021; Li et al., 2026), or representation learning, such as embedding (Zhang et al., 2025) or sequence-to-sequence models (Duquenne et al., 2023), these methods treat representation learning and data compression as disconnected tasks. On the other hand, we explore a paradigm of learning expressive representations that are compressed by design. In other words, we ask: how can we train a general-purpose lossy text reconstruction model that preserves semantic information while achieving a high compression rate? In summary, we make the following contributions: • We propose a Transformer-based (Vaswani et al., 2023) text autoencoder (AE) architecture that per-forms residual downscaling and upscaling of hidden representations along the time axis, uses a residual bottleneck projection, and quantization to produce compressed latent representations. We achieve 2.24 bits per byte compression rate using a 4.3B parameter autoencoder model on web text data which, to the best of our knowledge, is the highest compression rate for a text autoencoder model in literature. • We explore different quantization methods, training objectives, and datasets for autoencoder models with compressed latent representations and analyze their effects on the trained models, as well as evaluate the text reconstructions and representations on downstream question-answering and semantic 1arXiv:2610.10738v1 [cs.CL] 7 Oct 2026 text similarity tasks. We find that there is a reconstruction-representation trade-off, with our text autoencoder models favoring reconstruction over representation. • We show that common text similarity metrics, such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002), Rouge (Lin, 2004), chrF (Popović, 2015), and BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020), do not reliably measure semantic similarity between two texts, and we introduce and evaluate an LLM judge based on an adaptation of Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM; Lommel et al. 2024; Strandvik 2025). 

# 2 Related Work 

Lossless Text Compression. Common traditional approaches for lossless text compression include dictionary-based methods, such as the Lempel-Ziv algorithms and their variants (Ziv and Lempel, 1977, 1978), entropy coding methods (Huffman, 1952; Witten et al., 1987; Duda, 2013), and transforms (Robinson and Cherry, 1967; Ryabko, 1980; Burrows and Wheeler, 1994), often used in combination to reduce entropy and make the text more compressible (Deutsch, 1996; Collet and Kucherawy, 2021). On the other hand, neural-based loss-less text compression methods use neural networks to model sequences and compress the data using entropy coding (Knoll; Bellard, 2019, 2021; Valmeekam et al., 2023), with research mainly focusing on improving runtime efficiency and compression rates (Bellard, 2024; Mittu et al., 2024; Tacconelli, 2026). In the context of text compression, we explore lossy compression using neural-based autoencoder models which encode and reconstruct text that is semantically equivalent to the input text, and their rate-distortion trade-off. 

Context Compression. Improving efficiency of Transformer models over long text sequences was explored through the change of attention patterns to retain the expressivity while reducing the quadratic complex-ity (Beltagy et al., 2020; Ainslie et al., 2020; Zaheer et al., 2021), and alternative architectures whose state and computation does not grow with context length (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997; Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Gu et al., 2022; Gu and Dao, 2024; Yang et al., 2025a). Recent context compression research also explored representation of sequences using fewer latent tokens, such as through OCR (Wei et al., 2025) or text autoencoding-based methods (Liu and Qiu, 2025; Li et al., 2026), achieving up to 40x compression of the token sequence. Similar to autoencoding-based context compression methods, we use a Transformer-based autoencoder architecture to compress the model inputs along the time dimension into a compact latent rep-resentation from which the original output text can be reconstructed. We propose a new architecture which results in larger context compression rates. 

Text Embedding Models. Text embedding models encode a token sequence into a meaningful latent representation, commonly used for tasks such as semantic similarity, document retrieval, clustering, and classification (Muennighoff et al., 2023). Embedding models are usually obtained from Transformer-based language models trained on the unmasking (Devlin et al., 2019) or the next-token prediction task (Zhang et al., 2025), with the latent representation obtained by mean-pooling the latent token representations, or by adding a dedicated token to the sequence, such as the beginning-of-sequence or the end-of-sequence token. To further improve performance on downstream tasks, models are finetuned on task-specific data. While the goal of our work is not to create a text embedding model, we compare the latent representations of our compressive autoencoders to existing embedding models, as well as their text reconstruction capabilities. 

Text Reconstruction from Latent Representations. Morris et al. (2023) showed that latent represen-tations from text embedding models which were not originally trained for text reconstruction can be used to obtain text that is semantically close to the original through iterative text correction and generation. On the other hand, (Omni-)SONAR (Duquenne et al., 2023; Omnilingual SONAR Team et al., 2026) and Cideron et al. (2022) explore text reconstruction using autoencoder models where both the encoder and the decoder are trained on the text reconstruction task. Similar to (Omni-)SONAR and Cideron et al. (2022), we train a text autoencoder, while exploring latent representation compression and additional supervision objectives. 23 Method 

3.1 Experimental Setup 

In this section, we train 0.6B parameter Transformer-based autoencoder models with hidden dimension 768 and gated MLPs in the bfloat16 data type (16 bits per scalar). We use the cross-entropy loss, with model input and target tokens sampled from the Nemotron High-quality subset (Su et al., 2025) and tokenized using the Qwen3-0.6B tokenizer (Yang et al., 2025b). We use a batch size of 256K tokens, a peak learning rate of 

2 · 10 −4 with a 5% linear warmup followed by cosine decay, and the Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba, 2017). Following our initial experiments on a 1.4B parameter model, we allocate 24 tokens per parameter, at which we start to observe the loss converge, although we note that longer training continues to improve the loss. For evaluation of semantic similarity between the input and the reconstructed text, we report the 95% confidence intervals of the BLEU score (ranging between 0 and 1; Papineni et al. 2002) and the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B LLM judge score (ranging between 1 and 10; Qwen Team 2026a) with thinking enabled on 1000 validation documents following the training data distribution. Borrowing the terminology from the machine translation field, we use the input text as the reference and sample the reconstructions from our trained models using a temperature of 0.6 as the hypothesis, and compute the final score as the mean of the per-sample metric scores. We report the best result in bold and the second best result underlined. Beyond this, we provide more evaluation details in Section 4. 

3.2 Architecture 

Base Architecture. As our baseline, we use a Transformer-based autoencoder architecture which consists of: (1) Transformer encoder layers with the bidirectional attention mechanism, (2) pooling and bottleneck projection operations where the encoder output sequence is transformed into the final latent representation by either Mean-pooling or CLS-token pooling, followed by dimensionality-reducing MLP projection, and (3) Transformer decoder layers where an autoregressive (AR) Transformer reconstructs the text by cross-attending to the pooled and projected latent representation. Variants of this architecture are found in prior work, such as Liu and Qiu (2025) and Li et al. (2026). 

Multi-resolution Autoencoder Architecture. Inspired by Ronneberger et al. (2015), we propose the multi-resolution autoencoder architecture which introduces the following changes: (1) progressive scaling where the intermediates are progressively downscaled by the encoder and upscaled by the upscaler along the time dimension, removing the pooling operation, (2) residual bottleneck projection which reduces the dimensionality while preserving the residual path, and (3) upscaler supervision, with a non-autoregressive (non-AR) loss from the next-token prediction head applied to the upscaled intermediates. We show the multi-resolution autoencoder architecture in Figure 1. We implement progressive scaling along the time axis by replacing the standard residual MLP operation in the Transformer layer with a residual sequence rescaling operation of factor F that uses MLP F as shown in Algorithm 1, where MLP F is an MLP with input Xin ∈ RB×L×F D and output Xout ∈ RB×L×D. For example, multi-resolution layer with MLP 1 is equivalent to the standard Transformer layer, while using MLP 2 reduces the sequence length by 2. Since the scaling factor is multiplicative, stacking K multi-resolution layers of factors F1, . . . , F K results in the total scaling factor of Ftotal = QKi=1 Fi, which transforms the input input 

Xin ∈ RB×Ftotal L×D to the output Xout ∈ RB×L×D. We implement the residual bottleneck projection using an algorithm equivalent to Algorithm 1, with the only change being the axis along which the operation is performed.

3.3 Latent Representation Quantization 

Quantization Approaches. To further compress the latent representation, we introduce the quantization operation as the final step of the encoder, a standard practice in neural image (Ballé et al., 2016; Minnen et al., 2018) and audio compression (Zeghidour et al., 2021; Défossez et al., 2022). We evaluate both scalar and vector quantization approaches. Scalar quantization approaches map each scalar independently to its quantized value. For our experiments, we use finite scalar quantization (FSQ; Mentzer et al. 2023). On the other hand, vector quantization approaches map the whole vector to its quantized value using a codebook. For our experiments, we explore residual vector quantization (RVQ; Juang and Gray 1982) which sequentially applies the vector quantization operation on the residual of the previous step, product residual vector quantization (PRVQ) which performs RVQ on latent representation subspaces by treating a single vector as separate vector chunks, and chunked residual vector quantization (CRVQ) where we use an encoder with the same number of layers but a smaller total scaling factor and a narrower residual bottleneck projection, with RVQ applied to each of these latent representations. Specifically, CRVQ chunking results in one narrower latent representation per chunk of tokens defined by the total scaling factor. 

Experimental Results. We train 0.6B parameter multi-resolution AE models at sequence length 128 and 384 latent bottleneck dimension. We set the hyperparameters of each of the quantization methods to produce representations at approximately 9 bits per token to lie between the upper and lower extrema of the evaluation 4metrics. Specifically, we use FSQ with 8 values per scalar, RVQ with codebook size 4096 and 96 quantizers, PRVQ with codebook size 2048, 7 quantizers and 16 subspaces, and CRVQ with codebook size 2048, 7 quantizers, scaling factor 8 and latent dimension 24. For vector quantization approaches, we update the codebook as the exponential moving average of the quantizer inputs, use the commitment loss, and perform dead code rebalancing. Lastly, we include an experiment where we decrease the latent bottleneck dimension without using a quantizer as a baseline (Narrow Bottleneck). In Table 2, we observe that naively reducing the latent bottleneck dimension results in poor text reconstruction performance. Similarly, we observe that RVQ struggles to quantize high-dimensional vectors, which is improved by PRVQ. We see an improvement using FSQ and CRVQ, with CRVQ having the highest performance. We show additional CRVQ chunking hyperparameters in Table 8 (Appendix B). These results highlight the importance of the structure of the latent representations that the quantizers can exploit to turn into discrete codes. For example, we can draw a parallel to gzip+kNN text classification (Jiang et al., 2023) where the bzip2 compression algorithm shows larger compression rates than gzip but performs worse on a downstream classification task because bzip2 uses the Burrows-Wheeler transform (Burrows and Wheeler, 1994), permuting character order and discarding the structure that the distance metric needs.

3.4 Training Supervision 

We explore whether the models can be improved through different types of supervision at the loss, masking, and dataset levels. 5Loss Functions. In addition to the cross-entropy (CE) loss, we experiment with three loss functions: (1) bounded cross-entropy loss, BoundedCE b, where tokens with a loss smaller than b receive no gradient so that model capacity is spent on high loss tokens, i.e. mistakes, (2) upweighting the loss of the a desired token class to w, WeightedClass w, where we upweight the loss on numbers and proper nouns to invest more capacity on immutable information, and (3) including the language modeling loss, CE LM , to improve predictions of the decoder, with the cross-attention not being used and instead a learned vector being added. 

Token Masking. We mask the: (1) encoder inputs with the goal of learning better representations, and (2) decoder input prefixes as an alternative to the upscaler loss, both of which are studied in literature (Devlin et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2022). For encoder masking, we gradually cool down the proportion of the masked tokens at the end of training to adapt the model for inference. For decoder masking, we uniformly mask a prefix of the desired proportion of the sequences in the batch and keep the rest unmasked, without using the upscaler loss. 

Dataset Variants. In addition to our dataset with matching inputs and targets (Copy), we introduce two datasets: (1) round-trip translations (RTT) generated by machine translating data to German and back to English following Cideron et al. (2022), and (2) compressed text data (Compress) generated by asking an LLM to remove all redundant information from the sentences while preserving all semantic information. For both RTT and Compress, we use the original data as the input and the synthetic generation as the target. We provide more details in Appendix C. 

Experimental Results. We train 0.6B parameter multi-resolution AE models with the CRVQ quantizer at 9.63 bits per token using the same hyperparameters as in Section 3.3. In addition to BLEU and the judge score, we report the ratio of numbers (NumKept) and proper nouns (PNounKept) that are matched between the original and reconstructed texts. To identify numbers and proper nouns during both training and evaluation, we use spaCy part-of-speech tags obtained using the en_core_web_sm model (Honnibal et al., 2020). In Table 3, we observe that most of the proposed supervision methods do not beat the baseline using the standard CE loss with no masking on the Copy dataset. There are only two exceptions: encoder masking and the WeightedClass loss. However the WeightedClass loss trades off better immutable token class performance for worse overall reconstruction. Encoder masking likely improves performance due to the fact that the model cannot solely rely on copying the tokens directly inside the latent representation, and instead learns better representations for predictions based on the context. We show additional encoder masking experiments in Table 9 (Appendix B). Lastly, we observe that, while RTT and Compress with the upscaler loss perform worse than the Copy dataset, decoder masking improves reconstruction, indicating that the upscaler loss is too rigid when the input and the target tokens do not match.

4.1 Evaluation of Semantic Text Similarity 

Limitations of Traditional Metrics. We evaluate metrics commonly used in machine translation and text summarization for the purpose of evaluating semantic similarity between two texts. We compute BLEU (Pa-pineni et al., 2002), chrF (Popović, 2015), ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004), and BertScore (Zhang et al., 2020) on a set of reference-hypothesis pairs as the mean of the per-sample metric scores. Additionally, we compute the ratio of numbers (NumKept) and proper nouns (PNounKept) matched between the reference and the hypothesis as in Section 3.4, as well as a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-based (Qwen Team, 2026a) LLM judge score. We report the metrics across three datasets introduced in Section 3.4 (Copy, RTT, and Compress), and the Corrupt dataset, where we replace all words assigned the verb part-of-speech tag in the hypothesis of the Copy dataset with the string "run", thereby corrupting information. Analogous to detection of numbers and proper nouns, we detect verbs using the same spaCy setup. Table 4 demonstrates that, while these metrics track linguistic reconstruction, they can give high scores even when the underlying information is destroyed, as seen in the Corrupt dataset. On the other hand, using the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B judge correctly assigns high scores to RTT and Compress datasets, while giving a low score to the Corrupt dataset, indicating that high scores on surface-level text similarity metrics do not imply semantic similarity.

LLM Judge Formulation. For the LLM-based judge, we adapt the Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM; Lommel et al. 2024; Strandvik 2025) used in machine translation to detect information mismatches, specifically semantic mismatches, over-specification, under-specification, unwarranted addition, and omission. From these rules, we ask the judge to determine the number of semantic information pieces in the reference and hypothesis texts, count the number of differences between the two, and assign a text similarity score between 1 (lowest) and 10 (highest). By using these criteria, the judge assesses whether the reconstruction maintains the core meaning of the original text rather than just measuring surface-level overlaps. More details, including the prompt, are in Appendix D. 

LLM Judge Evaluation. We test the monotonicity of the judge scores with respect to information overlap between the references and the hypotheses. We generate 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90% summary datasets using models from Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026b) and Gemma4 (Gemma Team et al., 2026) model families of different sizes, and compute the token compression ratio for each of the summarized samples, using it as a proxy for ratio of information that was retained when summarizing. We compute the token compression ratio of each sample instead of using the target summary ratio because we find that the models do not always follow the target summary ratio. We show the results for the Qwen3.5-27B judge with thinking enabled on 1000 documents in Figure 3, highlighting that the judge scores monotonically increase both with the decrease in token compression ratio, as well as within the model family for larger models. To show that the judge generalizes across judge model families, we provide the results for the Gemma4-31B-it judge in Figure 7 (Appendix B).

Improving Lossy Compression. Although we achieve the best compression among neural lossy compres-sion methods, their lossy nature should allow for bitrates lower than neural lossless methods. We hypothesize that the supervised learning objective is not enough to reach these rates and that reinforcement learning (RL) might be needed. However, running RL with multiple rollouts and LLM-based semantic similarity objective is expensive and outside the scope of this initial work. One RL variant could be to generate only two rollouts per input and using a trained binary scorer to gradually steer the model to better generations, which we leave as future work. 

4.3 Downstream Task Evaluation 

Question-Answering Evaluation. To evaluate downstream utility of the text reconstructions of our models, we formulate a downstream question-answering task based on MS MARCO dataset (Bajaj et al., 2018). We pass the combined text passages from the MS MARCO dataset (Bajaj et al., 2018) through our text autoencoder models from Section 4.2 and SONAR at 128 sequence length, chunking and recombining sequences longer than 128 tokens. We then prompt the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model to extract the answer from the reconstructed passage based on the MS MARCO question, and prompt the same model to compare the extracted answer to the true MS MARCO answer. # 5 Conclusion 

We have shown that text autoencoders can achieve low bitrates comparable to traditional lossless compression methods while reconstructing semantically equivalent text. Our findings indicate that these models prioritize copying information over learning high-quality representations, presenting an opportunity for future work to improve autoencoder representations, which could in turn improve the compression rate. Additionally, to match higher neural lossless compression rates, it might be necessary to train by rewarding semantically close but not identical reconstructions. Given this, our proposed models are better suited for moderate compression and reconstruction over representation learning tasks, e.g., context compression or downstream tasks requiring reconstruction.
