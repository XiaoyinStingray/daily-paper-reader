## Abstract 

We propose MOSEL (Multi-Objective Stackelberg Efficient Learning), a framework for a posteriori multi-objective optimization (MOO) in deep neural networks that recovers a full front of Pareto stationary solutions at the computational cost of standard single-objective training. MOSEL reformulates the problem as a bilevel optimization problem that leverages network modularity to decouple representation learning from objective-preference alignment. Casting the bilevel problem as a Stackelberg game enables solving the original a posteriori MOO problem in a single forward–backward pass. As a result, MOSEL matches the time and memory efficiency of standard single-objective training while enabling scalable Pareto stationary front learning. Empirically, MOSEL uncovers diverse and optimal Pareto frontiers in strongly conflicting settings (e.g., fairness–accuracy). Remarkably, even in weakly conflicting regimes such as multi-task learning, it consistently converges to solutions closer to the utopia point, outperforming both standard single-objective training and specialized multi-task learning methods. These results highlight the broader potential of a posteriori MOO learning as a pathway to efficiently learn more diverse and robust representations, ultimately improving generalization. 

## 1 Introduction 

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) is ubiquitous across deep learning applications, arising whenever models must balance multiple objectives (i.e., losses during training). Many such objectives stem from competing stakeholder demands, leading to strongly conflicting objectives . For example, in 

fairness , individual and group fairness must be traded-off against predictive performance [48]; and, in 

LLM alignment , objectives such as helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty can conflict [21, 31, 51]. Similar conflicts emerge from the distributed or sequential nature of learning: in federated learning 

each client optimizes a local objective that may conflict with those of other clients [65]; and in 

reinforcement learning , agents must balance multiple reward signals simultaneously [19, 36, 70]. Other MOO applications lead to weak conflicts among objectives where, while the objectives may not be directly in conflict, the local nature of neural network (NN) optimization may lead to suboptimal solutions. For example, in multi-task learning (MTL) joint training can improve performance on individual tasks compared to training them independently (positive transfer); yet, task-specific losses may conflict during optimization, degrading overall performance [34, 56, 67]. Similar weak conflicts arise in multimodal learning [25, 63], and in domain generalization [5], often leading to suboptimal NNs that fail to capture diversity across modalities and domains. 

> Preprint. arXiv:2610.06397v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026

In practice, multiple objectives are often aggregated into a single scalar loss, a strategy that simplifies training but obscures trade-offs and can lead to suboptimal solutions. In particular, collapsing objectives into a single loss: (i) in strongly conflicting regimes, fails to capture the full spectrum of Pareto optimal solutions, thereby masking the trade-offs among objectives [19, 52]; or, (ii) in weakly conflicting regimes, requires manual weighting that is problem-dependent and sensitive to factors such as loss magnitudes [6]. Importantly, such a priori scalarization tends to bias optimization toward dominant objectives, e.g., prioritizing performance over fairness [43, 68] or favoring certain tasks over others [56, 67]. Consequently, it can lead to misleading interpretations of the underlying trade-offs and, more importantly, hinder the optimizer from reaching solutions close to the utopia point, thereby limiting overall performance. A principled solution to these issues is a posteriori 

MOO, and in particular, Pareto front learning (PFL). However, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no PFL approach that is suitable for training deep neural networks at scale, while providing Pareto stationarity guarantees .In this paper, we propose MOSEL (Multi-Objective Stackelberg Efficient Learning), the first frame-work for PFL in deep neural networks that recovers Pareto stationary solutions at scale. To that end, we exploit the modular structure of neural networks to decouple representation learning from objective preference alignment, enabling efficient MOO training without sacrificing scalability. Specifically, we introduce a framework for PFL that reformulates the problem as a bilevel optimization, which we then cast into a Stackelberg game, allowing the original problem to be solved efficiently with a single forward–backward pass of backpropagation. As a result, MOSEL achieves Pareto stationary learning with time and memory complexity comparable to conventional single-objective training .Beyond efficiency, we demonstrate that explicitly modeling multiple objectives allows MOSEL to: (i) recover diverse and high-quality Pareto frontiers in strongly conflicting settings; and, (ii) converge to solutions closer to the utopia solution in weakly conflicting regimes such as MTL, outperforming both single-objective baselines and specialized multi-task methods. These findings suggest that efficient multi-objective learning can serve as a foundation for learning richer, more diverse representations, potentially improving robustness and generalization in deep neural networks.

In this section, we introduce the key concepts from multi-objective and bilevel optimization that underpin our approach, and provide an overview of the related literature.

Related works. MOO methods can be categorized based on when preferences are specified [38, 44]. 

No-preference approaches resolve competing gradients without user input [13, 56]. A priori methods compute a single Pareto stationary solution for a fixed preference, typically via scalarization [33] or preference-aware gradient balancing [41], but require retraining for new preferences. A posteriori 

methods instead generate a set of trade-off solutions, e.g., by partitioning the objective space [32], tracing Pareto sets [40], or sampling preferences for coverage [4]. Interactive methods iteratively refine solutions with user feedback [44], but remain costly in deep learning, although approaches such as RLHF [7] and LLM alignment [47] can be seen as instances of this paradigm. A key limitation of both a priori and finite-solution a posteriori methods is their inability to generalize to unseen preferences without retraining. Pareto Front Learning (PFL) addresses this by learning a mapping from preferences to Pareto optimal solutions, aiming for (i) optimality (i.e., solutions lying on the Pareto front) and (ii) diversity (i.e., coverage of the full front) [9, 22]. Pareto Hypernetworks [45] achieve this but incur high computational and parameter costs due to per-objective gradients and auxiliary networks. COSMOS [53] improves scalability with a single training run and minimal overhead, but lacks guarantees of Pareto stationarity, as shown in Appendix A. In contrast to prior work, MOSEL combines the strengths of a posteriori methods and Pareto front learning while avoiding their key limitations, achieving Pareto stationary solutions without incurring significant computational or parameter overhead.

The bilevel formulation provides a more natural way to describe an objective that inherently depends on the outcome of a second objective [58], allowing for more flexibility in the optimization process by enabling separate optimization for each level. 

Related works. Previous works [4, 66] have explored bilevel formulations of MOO. Rather than fixing preference vectors a priori, [4] optimizes a distribution over a finite set of preferences in the upper level with respect to a quality metric (e.g., hypervolume) computed in the lower level. While this yields well-distributed solutions, the number of Pareto points remains fixed and limited by the upper-level design. In contrast, [66] propose a bilevel formulation with accuracy as the upper-level and fairness as the lower level objective. However, scaling this approach beyond two objectives is non-trivial, as each additional objective requires introducing additional hierarchical levels. In contrast to prior work, MOSEL leverages bilevel optimization to efficiently learn Pareto stationary fronts without restricting the number of solutions or objectives.

## 3 From a posteriori MOO to bilevel optimization of neural networks 

In this section, we cast the a posteriori MOO problem, or more specifically, the Pareto stationary front learning of deep neural networks into a bilevel optimization problem. To that end, we first assume a scalarization function , of the form s : Rm × [0 , 1] m 7 → R, to aggregate the vector of loss functions (i.e., objectives) L = ( L1, . . . , Lm) into a single scalar value, according to a given preference vector r ∈ [0 , 1] m. In our case, we consider linear scalarization Pmi=1 riLi(θ) [44] and smooth Tchebycheff scalarization 1 

> τ

log Pmi=1 exp ( τ · ri | L i(θ) − u⋆i |) [33]. Second, we leverage the modularity of neural networks to decouples the learning of optimal (i.e., Pareto stationary) from diverse (i.e., different trade-offs among objectives) solutions, as illustrated in Figure 1 (a). To this end, we partition the network into: (i) a representation learning network fθ,which seeks representations steering the Pareto front toward the utopia point, effectively guiding solu-tions toward optimality ; and (ii) a preference alignment network gϕ, which focuses on maintaining 

diversity of the solutions across the Pareto front. By separating optimality and diversity within the NN, MOSEL mitigates adversarial interactions between competing goals, yielding a more stable optimization process. In Section 4, we formalize this intuition by showing that stochastic preference sampling implicitly regularizes the representation network toward shared features, exponentially suppressing objective-specific ones. Importantly, this decomposition comes at almost no additional memory or computational cost, as it simply requires the incorporation of a preference vector as input to the preference alignment network and partitioning the NN computational graph during training. Figure 1 (b) illustrates this architectural design using the ResNet-34 architecture [20]. The network is divided into two parts: a representation learning network, comprising the convolutional stem and the first three residual layers, and a preference alignment network consisting of the fourth residual layer followed by the fully connected head. We leverage the intermediate representation after the third residual layer, denoted by z, as the conditioning point for preference injection. As highlighted in Figure 1 (b), the only modification to the original architecture is a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layer [50] inserted at the split point, which modulates z channel-wise based on the preference vector r, steering the shared representation towards the desired trade-off while preserving its spatial structure. The resulting conditioned representation z′ is subsequently passed through the remaining network. This design introduces only negligible overhead relative to the base network. ## 4 MOSEL: Multi-Objective Stackelberg Efficient Learning 

In this section, we introduce Multi-Objective Stackelberg Efficient Learning (MOSEL), a framework for a posteriori multi-objective optimization in deep neural networks that recovers a full front of Pareto stationary solutions at the computational cost of standard single-objective training. To that end, we build on recent works that leverage Stackelberg game theory for deep learning optimization—such as gradient homogenization in multi-task learning [24] and fairness–accuracy trade-offs [66]—to cast the bilevel optimization problem in Eq. (5) to a Stackelberg game. Following the notion of a differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium, introduced in [16], we train both the representation learning and preference alignment networks via simultaneous gradient updates—in other words, via a single forward-backward pass. A general Stackelberg game is a two-player game consisting of a leader and follower, where the leader plays first and the follower reacts optimally to this move, representing the hierarchical structure of bilevel optimization problems. The concepts of local Stackelberg equilibrium and differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium coincide except on a set of measure zero [16], making the differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium a natural target for gradient-based optimization. Fiez et al. [16] further show, that simultaneous gradient updates with a timescale separation, that is, a smaller learning rate for the leader than for the follower, is sufficient to ensure local convergence to a differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium. Intuitively, the faster follower updates keep θ close to its best response at every leader step, approximating the hierarchical structure of the Stackelberg game without requiring exact best-response computation. These guarantees directly connect to the Pareto stationarity condition in Eq. (3), as any differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium (ϕ⋆, θ⋆) satisfy their first-order stationarity conditions, which coincide with the condition in Eq. (3). Thus, convergence to a differentiable Stackelberg equilibrium implies convergence to a Pareto stationary solution, providing the theoretical foundation for the MOSEL algorithm in Alg. 1. 5.1 Strongly conflicting setting: Fairness-Performance trade-off 

Fairness in machine learning is inherently a multi-objective problem, as metrics such as Difference in Equality of Opportunity (DEO) directly conflict with predictive performance [48, 62]. Existing approaches typically treat fairness as a constraint or penalty, reducing the problem to a single trade-off and limiting the information available to decision makers [37]. This is particularly problematic in high-stakes domains such as criminal justice, credit scoring, and healthcare [43, 62]. We leverage this use case in the following experiments to empirically validate that MOSEL recovers diverse and high-quality Pareto frontiers in strongly conflicting settings. 

Datasets. We evaluate on Adult [2], COMPAS [1], and Credit [23], three widely used tabular fairness benchmarks with ’sex’ as the sensitive attribute, and on the image dataset FairFace [27], predicting ’gender’ under sensitive attribute ’race’ . Training objectives are binary cross-entropy (BCE) as a proxy for accuracy and a differentiable hyperbolic tangent relaxation of DEO [48] as a fairness surrogate; at test time we report misclassification error and DEO. 

Baselines and metrics. We compare MOSEL against COSMOS [53], evaluating both on 25 uniformly distributed test rays. We additionally compare against the post-processing baseline of Hardt et al. [18], which operates on an unconstrained classifier (using the same NN architecture) by sweeping a shared target TPR across per-group ROC convex hulls to derive group-specific decision thresholds, tracing out the Pareto front. Further details on hyperparameters, architectures, and training are provided in Appendix B. We report the relative hypervolume [71], defined as the achieved hypervolume divided by the maximum possible hypervolume between the utopia point (ideal, generally unattainable) and nadir point (worst per-objective value among observed solutions) as shown in Appendix B.2; alongside the number of non-dominated solutions per method, where dominated solutions are removed in post-processing. 

Results. Table 1 summarizes the results on both training and test objectives. MOSEL-LS and MOSEL-CS consistently match or outperform COSMOS across datasets in terms of both hypervolume and the number of Pareto optimal solutions, providing strong empirical evidence that MOSEL recovers diverse and high-quality Pareto frontiers in strongly conflicting settings. This advantage is particularly pronounced in Figure 2, where on FairFace, COSMOS collapses to a near-degenerate front, while both MOSEL variants maintain high hypervolume and a substantially larger set of Pareto optimal solutions. The post-hoc fairness baseline [18] remains competitive in hypervolume in some cases (e.g., Default and FairFace test error), but generally yields fewer non-dominated solutions. 5.2 Weakly conflicting setting: Multi-Task Learning 

In multi-task learning, joint training can improve task performance via positive transfer, but task-specific losses may also induce conflicting gradients, degrading performance [56]. However, such conflicts are not guaranteed as they depend in diverse factors like the data, training dynamics, etc., placing MTL in a weakly conflicting regime. As a result, methods designed to resolve gradient conflicts—such as GradNorm [6], FAMO [35], NashMTL [46], and PCGrad [67] —can even harm performance [34, 57]. We use the MTL setting to demonstrate that MOSEL adapts to both regimes without prior knowledge of the conflict structure. First, in a simple two-task setting, we show that MOSEL converges to solutions closer to the utopia point by effectively leveraging positive transfer, outperforming both standard single-objective training and specialized multi-task learning methods without additional computational cost. In a second experiment, we demonstrate that MOSEL efficiently enables positive transfer in settings with many tasks (objectives). 

Datasets. We evaluate on three synthetic and one real-world benchmark. Multi-MNIST [54], Multi-Fashion and Multi-Fashion+MNIST [32] are synthetic multi-task benchmarks that are composed of two digits or clothing items based on the MNIST [30] and Fashion-MNIST [64] dataset,where the goal is to simultaneously classify both objects (top-left and bottom-right), using per-task cross-entropy as objectives. As real-world benchmark we consider the CelebA dataset [39], containing over 200,000 images of celebrities, annotated with 40 binary attributes.

PCGrad is evaluated on 5 preferences, as it requires retraining for every preference. While standard PCGrad uses equal weighting, we weigh the objectives based on these preferences to obtain diverse solutions. As other baselines do not support preference conditioning, we report the single obtained solution. To further assess scalability, we conduct an ablation on CelebA comparing correlated and uncorrelated task sets as the number of jointly optimized objectives grows (Figure 4), with tasks added incrementally and performance measured relative to single-task baselines. For correlated tasks (left panel), shared representations enable strong positive transfer, with accuracy improvements over the single-task baseline growing consistently as more objectives are added. For uncorrelated tasks (right panel), positive transfer is weaker, yet MOSEL still outperforms single-task training on the majority of objectives. The occasional degradations can be attributed to two factors: tasks with high single-task accuracy have little room for improvement, and by choosing the Pareto optimal solution closest to the zero vector for evaluation, high-loss tasks dominate the distance computation, while low-loss tasks (high single-task accuracy) contribute minimally. Nonetheless, the overall performance gains come at no additional cost: computational overhead remains negligible as the number of objectives grows, Figure 8 shows that runtime stays approximately constant, with only minor fluctuations; while memory increases only marginally, highlighting the scalability of MOSEL. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We propose an efficient a posteriori multi-objective framework for deep neural networks that achieves Pareto stationary solutions at the cost of standard single-objective training via a bilevel, Stackelberg formulation enabling single-pass updates. Empirically, it recovers diverse Pareto frontiers in strongly conflicting settings and converges closer to the utopia point in weakly conflicting regimes such as multi-task learning, demonstrating that efficient multi-objective learning scales and improves performance by learning more diverse representations to capture all objectives. 9Limitations and future work. Despite its advantages, our approach has several limitations that open avenues for future research. First, our results suggest that efficient multi-objective learning can yield richer, more robust representations, especially in weakly conflicting regimes, motivating further study on transfer [49] and alignment [21]. Moreover, our fairness–performance experiments show that our method achieves better trade-offs while providing diverse solutions for informed deploy-ment; extending this to other high-stakes domains such as clinical risk prediction [42], recidivism assessment [37] or credit scoring [29] is an important direction for future work. Second, the method relies on a modular decomposition of neural networks, which may not be straightforward to design or optimal for all architectures; exploring adaptive or learned decompositions is therefore another important avenue. Third, while the computational overhead of MOSEL for recovering a Pareto stationary front is minimal, deploying the model requires selecting a preference vector—and thus a specific trade-off. This decision becomes increasingly challenging as the number of objectives grows and remains an open problem [26]; addressing it via human-in-the-loop methods for multi-objective evaluation and selection is a key direction for future work. Overall, our results highlight the promise of our approach and, together with these challenges, motivate a broader research agenda at the intersection of scalable multi-objective learning, adaptive model design, and decision-aware deployment of deep neural models.

B.1 Experimental setup General settings. All datasets use a 70/10/20 train/validation/test split. We use early-stopping based on validation hypervolume across all methods. For a fair comparison, all methods share the same architecture. MOSEL additionally requires splitting the network into a representation learning and a preference alignment component, as described in the respective subsections below. For COSMOS, we use all hyperparameters as reported in the original paper. Unless stated otherwise, all methods share the same training hyperparameters, including the Dirichlet sampling parameter α, number of epochs, and batch size, which mostly follow the setup in [53]. All methods use Adam [28] as the optimizer. For MOSEL, we use separate Adam optimizers for the leader and follower. To ensure convergence to an equilibrium point, the leader learning rate must be smaller than the follower’s, we found that one order of magnitude difference works best. Based on that, we select the learning rate combination yielding the best validation hypervolume. Learning rates are decayed by a factor of 0.1 at dataset-specific milestones. For all other methods, hyperparameters are selected via grid search over validation hypervolume. The smooth Tchebychev scalarization [33] additionally requires a smoothing parameter τ , set to 0.01 across all datasets, and the utopia point u⋆. Since u⋆ is generally unknown, we approximate it with a running minimum over objectives observed during training. All experiments were conducted on an internal computing cluster with an automatic job assignment system, thus we cannot ensure the specific CPU and GPU used for each particular experiment. However, all jobs were submitted with defined resource requirements: multi-dataset experiments requested 2 GPUs and 1 CPU, FairFace and CelebA experiments requested 2 GPUs and 4 CPUs, and tabular experiments required no GPU and 2 CPUs. The required memory for each experiment is reported in the respective tables. 

B.1.1 Tabular datasets Model. For the fairness use case, we consider three tabular datasets: Adult, COMPAS and Default. Following prior work [53], we use an MLP with two hidden layers and hidden dimensions 60 and 25: 

Linear( d, 60) → ReLU → Linear(60, 25) → ReLU → Linear(25, 1) .

Hyperparameters. The model is trained for 50 epochs with a batch size of 256, using α = [0 .5, 0.5] 

for Adult and COMPAS and α = [0 .1, 0.5] for Default, following the values of [53]. The COSMOS learning rate is 10 −3 for all tabular datasets without a learning rate scheduler. For MOSEL, we use leader and follower learning rates of [10 −3, 10 −2] for Adult and Default, and [10 −2, 10 −1] for COMPAS. The learning rate is decayed by a factor of 0.1 at epochs 15 and 40 for Adult, epochs 30 and 40 for COMPAS, and epochs 30 and 45 for Default. 

Splitting the architecture. This architecture provides two natural split points, namely before the second or third linear layer. Empirically, splitting before the last linear layer yields insufficient capacity in the preference alignment network to meaningfully incorporate the preference vector. Thus we split before the second linear layer across all datasets. 

B.1.2 Multi-Image classification Model. For the Multi-MNIST, Multi-Fashion and Multi-Fashion+MNIST datasets we adopt the standard LeNet architecture [30]. 

Hyperparameters. Following the values of [53], the model is trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 256 and α = [1 .2, 1.2] . The COSMOS learning rate is 10 −3, decayed by a factor of 0.1 at epochs 20, 40, 80, and 90. For MOSEL, we use leader and follower learning rates of [10 −4, 10 −3]

with the same scheduler milestones. A weight decay of 10 −3 is applied to the leader to keep the impact of leader and follower balanced and improve training stability. 

Splitting the architecture. We split the network immediately after flattening, using the convolu-tional trunk for representation learning which produces a 720-dimensional embedding z, that is then passed to the subsequent shared linear layer and two task-specific heads that form the preference alignment network. 20 B.1.3 CelebA Model. For CelebA, we use the pretrained EfficientNet-B4 [61]. 

Hyperparameters. Again, we follow the values of [53], thus the model is finetuned for 30 epochs with a batch size of 32 and α = [1 , 1] . The COSMOS learning rate is 5 × 10 −4, for MOSEL, we use leader and follower learning rates of [5 × 10 −5, 5 × 10 −4].

Splitting the architecture. The modular block structure allows for many candidate split points. We select the split parameter by cross-validation on validation hypervolume. The optimal split was found after the 16th MBConv block, such that the convolutional stem and first 16 MBConv blocks form the representation learning network. The remaining MBConv blocks together with the convolutional head, pooling, and two-layer MLP task heads form the preference alignment network. 

B.1.4 FairFace Model. For FairFace we use a pretrained ResNet-34 [20] following [27]. 

Hyperparameters. Since FairFace was not used in [53], all hyperparameters are selected via cross-validation on validation hypervolume. The model is finetuned for 30 epochs with a batch size of 128 and α = [0 .5, 0.5] . The COSMOS hyperparameters are λ = 0 .1 and learning rate of 10 −4,decayed by a factor of 0.1 at epochs 20. For MOSELwe use leader and follower learning rates of 

[10 −5, 10 −4] with the same scheduler milestones. 

Splitting the architecture. Possible split points are restricted to boundaries between residual stages (layer1 - layer4), which gives a total of four possible split poitns. Again, we cross-validate this parameter on validation hypervolume. The optimal split was found after layer3, thus the convolutional stem together with the first three residual stages form the representation learning network, and layer4 together with the two-layer MLP task heads form the preference alignment network.

Figure 5: Hypervolume computation The hypervolume indicator measures the vol-ume of the objective space dominated by a set of solutions and bounded by a reference point [71]. As illustrated in Fig. 5, we compute it stepwise as a sum of rectangles, where each solution on the approximated Pareto front contributes a rectangular region bounded by its objective values and the nadir point. To enable compari-son across datasets and methods, we report the 

relative hypervolume , defined as the ratio of the achieved hypervolume (shown in blue) to the maximum possible hypervolume enclosed between the utopia and nadir points (shown in gray). The nadir point is set per dataset as the largest objective value observed across all meth-ods plus a margin of 0.05, ensuring all solutions are strictly dominated. The utopia point is task-dependent: for multi-task learning it is set to (0,0), representing zero loss or zero error on all tasks, while for fairness it is set to (min er-ror/min loss, 0), reflecting the best achievable error/loss at zero discrimination. A relative hy-pervolume of 1 thus corresponds to a solution set that perfectly covers the entire trade-off region between the utopia and nadir points. Note that the utopia point is in general not achievable in multi-objective optimization, as it would minimize all objectives simultaneously. 21 B.3 Conditioning on the preference vector 

For the tabular datasets, we concatenate the preference vector r with the learned representation 

z and pass both directly to the preference alignment network, which is sufficient when no spatial structure is present. For image data on the other hand, we condition on the preference vector r using a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layer [50], which has been shown to enable effective preference-dependent representation modulation in MOO settings [4, 14]. Concretely, a linear layer projects the preference vector r to channel-wise scale and shift parameters (γ, β), which modulate the representation z channel-wise as z′ := γ(r)⊙z +β(r). This requires adding a single linear layer 

Linear( dim (r), 2 · dim (z)), introducing only 2 · dim (z) · (dim (r) + 1) additional parameters, which are a negligible overhead relative to the full network.

## D Ablations 

D.1 Dirichlet parameter α

To isolate the effect of the Dirichlet parameter α on the quality and diversity of the recovered Pareto front, we conduct an ablation study by varying α across four datasets, two from each use case, under both linear scalarization (LS) and Chebyshev scalarization (CS). Results are reported in Table 6. The sensitivity to α varies across datasets and scalarization functions. In strongly conflicting settings, performance degrades substantially as α increases, particularly under LS: on Adult, HV drops from 

0.939 at α = 0 .5 to 0.816 at α = 1 .2, while on COMPAS the decline is even more dramatic (from 

0.920 to 0.164 ), indicating a near-total failure to discover diverse trade-off solutions when preference samples concentrate near the simplex center. Under CS, Adult follows a similar trend (HV from 

0.819 to 0.524 ), whereas COMPAS remains remarkably stable with HV above 0.920 across all α

values. In contrast, the two MTL datasets are largely insensitive to α: on Multi-Fashion, HV and HV MCR remain effectively constant, and on Multi-Fashion+MNIST, only a mild upward trend is observed as α increases. Taken together, these results highlight two key findings. First, the impact of α is most pronounced on datasets where the objectives are strongly conflicting, and where the scalarization function is sensitive to the geometry of the preference simplex (LS more so than CS). Second, even on tasks where hypervolume remains stable, increasing α systematically contracts the extreme solutions, reducing the diversity of the recovered front—an effect that aggregate metrics can obscure. For the MTL setting, this behavior aligns with the theoretical analysis in Appendix A.2, which shows that smaller α values (below 1) increase the variance of stochastic preference sampling, strengthening the implicit bias toward learning features shared across objectives and thereby promoting greater Pareto-front diversity. On the strongly conflicting benchmarks, the effect of α is empirically even more pronounced, yet falls outside the assumptions of our current theoretical framework—extending the analysis to such settings remains an interesting direction for future work. 27 F Convergence analysis 

To verify that preference conditioning does not introduce optimization instability, we compare the convergence behavior of MOSEL-LS against an equal weight baseline on Multi-MNIST. Both methods use an identical optimizer and learning rate schedule (standard Adam). For each epoch we record the maximum loss across all sampled preference vectors, capturing worst-case optimization behavior. Results are reported in Table 9 as means and standard deviations over 5 seeds. Both methods exhibit nearly identical convergence profiles: a rapid descent during the first 10 epochs followed by a stable plateau, with no signs of divergence or increasing instability. After the initial transient, MOSEL maintains a small, constant gap in maximum loss relative to the baseline (∆ ≈ +0 .09 from epoch 10 onward). This gap is expected, as MOSEL repeatedly optimizes under extreme preference vectors (encouraged by our choice of α < 1) in order to recover a diverse Pareto front, rather than a single fixed weighting. Despite this more challenging optimization setting, MOSEL achieves tighter cross-seed variance than the baseline ( ±0.02 vs. ±0.04 at convergence), and the constant magnitude of ∆ after epoch 10 confirms that the additional complexity introduced by preference conditioning does not compound over training. These results demonstrate that MOSEL preserves the optimization stability of conventional single-objective training while successfully learning to cover the Pareto front.
