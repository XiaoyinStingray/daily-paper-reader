# ABSTRACT 

Recent theoretical work identified fundamental properties of representational ge-ometry that shape inference ability of deep neural networks. These include signal-noise factorization (SNF), the ability to segregate signal from noise, and signal-signal factorization (SSF), the ability to segregate task-specific and task-irrelevant signals. Here, we built new regularizers that explicitly reinforce these two prop-erties during training. We compared networks trained with these regularizers to 

L2-regularized baseline networks on the CIFAR-100 image classification task to understand how our new regularizers shape representation geometry and impact performance on a well-known computer vision baseline. Enhancing SNF via regu-larization improved model performance but enhancing SSF did not. Motivated by biomedical diagnostics applications, we next investigated how our new regulariz-ers affected performance on the BloodMNIST dataset treated with MedMNIST-C corruptions at five severity levels, and found even larger performance gains using the SNF regularizer. To understand the mechanism by which signal-noise fac-torization produces improved performance, we analyzed the nuisance subspaces across regularization regimes, finding that the SNF-regularized models represent noise in distinct subspaces, separate from class-relevant signal. Because this ge-ometry is explicit, the dominant corruption-induced directions can be estimated on held-out data and projected out of the representations. This manipulation led to a substantial gain in categorization accuracy. These results demonstrate that regular-izers that enforce signal-noise factorization can produce substantial improvements on computer vision tasks that contain out-of-distribution image distortions at in-ference time. They also highlight how explicitly shaping representations affects model performance: isolating nuisance variables from categorical ones is more important than maintaining factorized representations of categorical variables. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Deep neural networks learn complex representations across a wide range of domains to solve prob-lems. In most applications, a useful representation must support generalization, transfer, and reliable readout, yet the field is only beginning to understand how the geometric or statistical properties of neural representations affect these outcomes. Rather than asking how to improve performance di-rectly, we ask: how do specific structural properties of hidden representations shape downstream task performance? We answer this by treating regularization as a manipulation to alter two geomet-ric properties identified by a recent theoretical study as the critical ones governing generalization (Wakhloo et al., 2026): signal-signal factorization (SSF), the segregation of task-specific from task-irrelevant signals, and signal-noise factorization (SNF), the segregation of signal from noise. Across primate visual cortex, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, populations encode task-relevant information in one subspace while segregating task-irrelevant variation into partially orthogonal subspaces (Lindsey & Issa, 2024; Bernardi et al., 2020; Whittington et al., 2019). To this end, we implement two regularizers targeting the SSF and SNF properties. We apply these penalties to the penultimate layer of ResNet-18 networks (He et al., 2015) trained on CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009) and on the biomedically-motivated BloodMNIST classification task (Yang et al., 2023), and compare against unregularized and L2-regularized baselines. Beyond measuring accuracy and robustness to corrupted samples, we also analyze the representational structure each regularizer produces, asking not only whether factorization-based regularization helps, but by what mechanism. This analysis reveals that regularizing to improve signal-noise factorization improves categorization performance on samples with out-of-distribution distortions by segregating the ef-fects of the distortions into a subspace of the representation that can be ignored by the readout during inference. For contrast, regularizing to improve signal-signal factorization had little impact on categorization performance. 1.1 RELATED WORK 

A central question in representation learning is which geometric and statistical properties of hidden-layer activations determine downstream task performance. A substantial body of work characterizes the structure of learned representations after training, by measuring effective dimensionality (Abbas et al., 2021), and the alignment of class-conditional geometry with linear classifiers (Papyan et al., 2020). Probing classifiers (Belinkov, 2021) similarly query frozen representations for task-relevant structure. These methods are diagnostic in that they describe what a representation contains, but do not manipulate it. Our approach is complementary. By introducing targeted regularizers during training, we produce representations with controlled structural properties, enabling a more causal account of how those properties affect readout accuracy. A parallel line of analytical work asks why gradient descent and weight decay produce the particular solutions they do (Neyshabur et al., 2014; Gunasekar et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2022; Kou et al., 2023). Neural collapse theory has established that weight decay is not merely incidental but necessary for cross-entropy-trained networks to reach a maximally class-separated, within-class-collapsed geometric optimum (Papyan et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2021), and related work shows that weight decay drives representational isotropy and cross-class orthogonality (Kulkarni et al., 2026). Critically, this effect is not monolithic: weight decay appears to act through several distinct mechanisms operating simultaneously (Zhang et al., 2018; Yunis et al., 2024). These results are observational, in that geometry is measured in networks trained with weight decay and correlated with performance. We ask instead whether these geometric properties can be optimized explicitly and separately, and in doing so isolate their individual effects on network behavior. Another class of methods reshape representations through objectives defined on activation statistics. Self-supervised methods such as Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al., 2021) and VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022) penalize covariance across feature dimensions to prevent representational collapse, targeting label-independent structure. Domain adaptation methods enforce representational invariance across environments, either adversarially (Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015), through moment matching (Sun & Saenko, 2016), or through invariant-predictor objectives (Arjovsky et al., 2020). These methods modify representations in service of a downstream objective, like generalization or transfer, without isolating which structural change drives the observed effect. The regularizers considered here are instead designed to manipulate specific, interpretable geometric properties of the class-conditional distribution, so that the link between representation structure and performance can be studied di-rectly.

# 3 MODEL TRAINING AND ARCHITECTURE 

To evaluate our regularizers across different classification tasks, we use two datasets: CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), a standard benchmark enabling comparison to existing literature and confident measurement of model geometry, and BloodMNIST (Yang et al., 2023), a benchmark biomedically-motivated classification task with substantial inter- and intra-class nuisance variation. In both settings, we use a ResNet-18 architecture with CrossEntropy loss, applying our geometric regularizers to the penultimate layer. For each dataset, we perform a joint hyperparameter sweep over L2 strength and SSF/SNF regularization strength, selecting values by validation performance; we compare against unregularized and L2-only baselines, with the L2 strength for these baselines selected the same way. Full training configurations, including optimizer settings, learning rate sched-ules, augmentation policies, and compute resources, are provided in App. A.2.3. 

# 4 MODEL AND REGULARIZER EVALUATION 

In this section, we describe the methods used to analyze regularizer impact on model geometry and performance. 4.1 MODEL GEOMETRY 

To understand the regularizers’ effects on model geometry, we compute Wakhloo’s s and f (Eq. 3 and 4 respectively) metrics on our model’s penultimate activations, evaluating the degree to which the goal of each geometric regularizer is achieved across CIFAR-100-trained models. 4.2 MODEL PERFORMANCE 

We measure whether improvements in representational geometry confer greater performance by first measuring baseline test accuracy on the unseen CIFAR-100 and BloodMNIST test sets. We then measure performance on out-of-distribution examples for CIFAR-100 by measuring each model’s ability to transfer performance across corruption strengths by independently training models with weak and strong augmentations and evaluating each model on augmented images of the opposite strength. On the BloodMNIST dataset, we assess the ability to perform on out-of-distribution ex-amples by applying MedMNIST-C corruptions (Di Salvo et al., 2024) to the unseen test set and comparing the performance of each model across corruption severity and corruption type. On the BloodMNIST dataset, we also report the sensitivity and specificity under corruption. 4.3 MECHANISTIC SUBSPACE ANALYSIS 

To probe the mechanism by which SNF regularization improves performance, we wanted to de-termine whether nuisance variation was represented in a separate subspace. We extracted 512-dimensional penultimate representations ( Z) from each trained model on the BloodMNIST test set under clean conditions and all 55 corruption variants (11 types × 5 severities). We then identified the principal directions of corruption-induced variation as follows. For each model, we formed the perturbation matrix ∆ = Zcorrupted − Zclean and ran a truncated SVD (top-50 components) on the stacked ∆ across all 55 variants. # 5 RESULTS 

5.1 REPRESENTATION GEOMETRY AND CIFAR-100 PERFORMANCE 

To test our regularizers on a widely used dataset, we train models on the CIFAR-100 dataset (Krizhevsky et al., 2009). We first measure f (Fig. 2a), the theoretical quantity which measures whether class representations occupy orthogonal subspaces. Within both the No-L2 and With-L2

groups, we observe that SSF regularization produces the largest f , SNF regularization produces an intermediate increase, and unregularized or L2-only representations show the smallest quantity. This pattern is mirrored in s (Fig. 2b), the theoretical measure of noise in the signal subspace: SSF regu-larization again maximizes s most aggressively, while SNF regularization provides an intermediate increase relative to no regularization and L2 alone. We next apply our CIFAR-100-trained models on the held out CIFAR-100 test set (Fig. 2c), where SNF regularization improves performance over no regularization, and when applied jointly with 

L2, achieves the best overall accuracy among all conditions tested. SSF regularization, in contrast, does not improve generalization and consistently leads to lower accuracy than the unregularized baseline and its L2-paired counterpart. The combination of strong geometric regularization from SSF regularization paired with weaker test performance is consistent with two directions of prior work. First, neural collapse theory demonstrates that in the final layers of training, neural networks already aim to orthogonalize class means, and our results demonstrate that an additional penalty as regularization is unlikely to add to network performance (Neyshabur et al., 2014; Gunasekar et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2022; Kou et al., 2023). Secondly, while enforcing signal orthogonality via structural loss functions enhances component interpretability, it risks over-constraining the optimization landscape. Real-world signal attributes are rarely perfectly disjoint, and by forcing an orthogonal basis, a model may prioritize structural constraints over data fidelity, producing a rigid solution space that discards subtle, highly discriminative inter-class variation (Yan 5Preprint. To determine why SNF regularization produces better performance on corrupted examples, we car-ried out a mechanistic analysis of each model’s representation structure. Fig. 4a shows the cumulative variance of the perturbation matrix ∆ explained by an increasing num-ber of principal components. The L2+SNF regularized model requires substantially more components (33) to reach the same threshold, indicating that SNF regularization causes corruption perturbations to occupy a higher-rank subspace ( reff =11.38 ±0.26 versus 11.17 ±0.67 and 24.75 ±2.48 for L2, L2+SSF, and 

L2+SNF, respectively). At first, this is counterintuitive, as a regularizer intended to suppress noise appears instead to spread it out. Surprisingly, this higher rank reflects nuisance variance being separated from the signal subspace, not a failure to suppress noise. The Ls regularizer (Eq. 6) penalizes noise-subspace variance specifi-cally within the signal subspace Φ, placing no constraint on variance elsewhere. To test whether the elevated rank under SNF regularization reflects this asymmetry, we decomposed corruption-induced variance into components inside and outside the signal subspace (App. A.2.9). For L2 and L2+SSF regularization, corruption-induced variance is split 61.0%/39.1% and 57.9%/42.2% between the sig-nal subspace and its complement (Fig. 4b), indicating that a majority of nuisance variation remains entangled with class-discriminative directions under both regimes. Under L2+SNF regularization, this relationship inverts: only 16.3% of corruption-induced variance falls within the signal subspace, with the remaining 83.7% displaced into its orthogonal complement. This asymmetry indicates that SNF regularization does not suppress corruption-induced variance globally. It instead moves that variance out of the signal subspace, where it is free to occupy whatever structure is present in the remaining, unconstrained directions of activation space. The elevated rank in Fig. 4a is therefore ev-idence that successful eviction leaves noise occupying a higher-dimensional region of the orthogonal complement, which carries no constraint on its own rank. To test whether the nuisance variation subspace could be identified and removed, we projected out the top-k singular vectors of ∆ from each corrupted representation and reclassified the test samples using the frozen linear head, sweeping k ∈ { 0, 5, . . . , 50 } (Fig. 4c). Estimating and evaluating this subspace on the same corrupted test data yields substantial recovery for L2+SNF regularized model (plateauing at 76.43% by k = 10 ) but not for L2 or L2+SSF regularized ones, whose accuracy in-stead declines with increasing k, consistent with Fig. 4c. This estimate is optimistic, however, since part of the subspace is fit to the same images used to evaluate it. To test whether the identified sub-space instead reflects a stable property of the model, we re-estimated the top-k singular vectors of ∆

on the corrupted validation split and applied this fixed subspace to project noise out of test-set repre-sentations before classification (Fig. 4d). For L2+SNF regularized model, this validation-estimated projection improves test accuracy from 60.9 ±0.6% at k = 0 to 69.3 ±0.8% at k = 10 , an improve-ment of ∼8.4% ( ∼13.8% relative), before gradually declining at larger k. The smaller performance recovery here, relative to the test-fit estimate above, is expected given the independence of the two splits, and is the more meaningful of the two figures, demonstrating that the noise subspace identi-fied for the L2+SNF regularized model is a property of that transfers across independent samples, rather than an estimation artifact. For L2 and L2+SSF regularized models, the analogous validation-estimated projection does not recover performance and instead degrades it further with increasing k,consistent with the entangled geometry indicated by panels b and c. Together, these results indicate that SNF regularization produces representations in which corruption-induced noise is actively displaced from the signal subspace into a separate, identifiable subspace that is largely corruption-specific (Fig. A.3). We also find that projecting out the nuisance variation can reliably improve performance on unseen, corrupted samples. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we bridged the regularization-interpretability literature with explicit mechanistic reg-ularizers that directly target geometric properties of learned representations. We showed that L2

regularization operates through multiple distinct mechanisms, a finding reflected in our own ex-periments and consistent with prior literature on weight decay’s diverse effects (Neyshabur et al., 2014; Gunasekar et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2022; Kou et al., 2023). Explicitly regularizing for signal-signal factorization (SSF) did not yield consistent performance benefits. In contrast, regularizing for signal-noise factorization (SNF) produced meaningful gains, particularly under distribution shift, a benefit that stems from SNF regularization’s ability to orthogonalize nui-sance variation away from signal-carrying directions, enabling substantial performance recovery on 8Preprint. Under review. out-of-distribution samples. Together, these results identify a mechanism by which explicit signal-noise factorization can be directly targeted for improving out-of-distribution robustness. 6.1 BROADER IMPACTS 

These findings carry practical relevance beyond benchmark robustness. Machine learning perfor-mance in many domains is constrained by nuisance variation: SNF regularization seems to be par-ticularly useful in these situations. In medical imaging, models trained at one hospital often gener-alize poorly to others, since hospital-specific scanner and staining artifacts become entangled with the diagnostic signal (Zech et al., 2018). A regularizer that explicitly separates the two offers a path toward diagnostic tools that generalize more reliably across clinical sites. 6.2 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

We note several limitations of this work. First, our comparisons are limited to a small set of reg-ularizers as we have not benchmarked against every possible regularization regime. Second, all experiments use a single architecture (ResNet-18), and we cannot say with certainty whether these effects hold for architectures with substantially different inductive biases, such as vision transform-ers or architectures without residual connections. Third, our evaluation spans only two datasets. We believe that this limitation is at least partially mitigated by CIFAR-100 and BloodMNIST rep-resenting quite different domains (natural object recognition versus medical microscopy) such that consistent trends across both offer some initial evidence of generality. Nevertheless, broader vali-dation across additional datasets, modalities, and distribution-shift types is an important avenue for future research. Future work should explore several directions. First, we tested SSF and SNF regularization indepen-dently. Jointly applying both may reveal interactions that neither term captures alone, particularly given the coupling we observed between the two penalties even under independent regularization. Second, our experiments focus on supervised classification. Extending SNF regularization to rep-resentation learning settings, such as self-supervised or contrastive pretraining, may be especially promising given that these methods already rely on shaping representation geometry, and explicit signal-noise factorization could offer a principled complement to existing objectives like variance-covariance regularization. Third, the asymmetry we observe where L2 shapes both factorization properties but the reverse does not hold warrants theoretical analysis to characterize precisely which components of L2’s effect are captured by SSF/SNF geometry and which reflect genuinely distinct mechanisms.

A.2.1 ARCHITECTURE DETAILS 

For the CIFAR-100 task, we use the standard torchvision ResNet-18 backbone with randomly ini-tialized weights. The final fully connected layer contains 100 output neurons for CIFAR-100’s 100 output classes. For the BloodMNIST task, we use 64 ×64 input images and confirm that the final fully connected layer contains 8 output dimensions for the 8 classes. We train both models from random initialization. We apply L2 regularization to model weights across all layers of models. The SNF regularizer is applied to the penultimate layer, the 512-dim activation immediately preceding the final fully con-nected layer. An additional BatchNorm layer (with learnable γ and β) is inserted immediately before the SNF penalty is applied. This layer is present in all conditions, including the unregularized and 

L2-only baselines, and should be understood as a fixed architectural choice rather than a component of the regularization scheme. A.2.2 STATISTICAL REPORTING 

All models were trained with n = 3 random seeds (1, 2, 3), with results reported as mean ±standard deviation unless otherwise noted. Given the small number of seeds, we perform no formal statistical tests in this work and avoid inferential language in the text. Instead we rely on per-seed paired differences, which we treat as more informative than a p-value at this sample size. A.2.3 CIFAR-100 TRAINING DETAILS 

Models were trained with stochastic gradient descent (momentum 0.9) and a CosineAnnealing learn-ing rate schedule (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017). Because the SNF regularizer requires inverting the per-batch label covariance Ω, and therefore requires every class to be present in every batch, we use a StratifiedBatchSampler with batch size 1350. Learning rate was scaled to account for the in-creased batch size, from 0.0579 to 0.579 (Goyal et al., 2018). Training used an expanded set of aug-mentations including Pad(4, reflect), RandomHorizontalFlip(p=0.5), RandomRotation(degrees=15), ColorJitter(brightness=0.4, contrast=0.4, saturation=0.4), CIFAR-100-specific normalization, Ran-domErasing(p=0.5, scale=(0.02, 0.33), ratio=(0.3, 3.3), value=0). Models were trained on the UCLA HPC cluster using 1 NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU to accommodate large-batch training. A.2.4 CIFAR-100 AUGMENTATION TEST 

To test whether our regularizers allow transfer of nuisance variation across augmentation strengths, we train models using two mutually exclusive augmentation strengths (the strong augmentation ranges do not include the weak augmentation ranges) and test the models on augmentations of the opposite strength. To this end, we train the models using the fixed hyperparameters identified in App. A.2.6 and use the following augmentations:

Model training used the same setup as the original MedMNIST paper: 100 epochs, batch size 112 (original MedMNIST uses 128, we use 112 to satisfy the StratifiedBatchSampling objective), and learning rate 1 × 10 −3 with multi-step decay to 1 × 10 −4 at epoch 50 and 1 × 10 −5 at epoch 75 (Yang et al., 2023). We used an L2 weight of 1 × 10 −6, the minimum required to reach performance equivalent to the original MedMNIST results. Training included the augmentations used in the 14 Preprint. Under review. For each dataset (CIFAR-100 and BloodMNIST), hyperparameters were searched individually rather than jointly. We first swept L2 strength alone on the unregularized network and selected the value that maximized validation accuracy. This value was then held fixed while separately sweeping SNF regularization strength (for L2+SNF) and SSF regularization strength (for L2+SSF), so as to isolate the benefit each regularizer confers on top of the selected L2 baseline. On CIFAR-100, we additionally evaluated SNF and SSF regularization in isolation, with L2 strength fixed at 0, to assess their effect independent of weight decay.
