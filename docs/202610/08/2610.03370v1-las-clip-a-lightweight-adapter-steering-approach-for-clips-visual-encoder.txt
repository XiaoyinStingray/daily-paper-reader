Abstract. CLIP’s visual encoder produces only global image represen-tations, limiting its use in region-level tasks. Existing adaptations rely on visual prompting, input masking, or encoder fine-tuning, each compro-mising pre-trained representations. We propose LAS-CLIP, a Lightweight Adapter Steering approach that keeps every CLIP parameter frozen. A compact MaskAdapter generates per-head, per-layer attention biases from an input mask and injects them into the frozen self-attention layers, steering attention toward the target region. Crucially, because the back-bone remains strictly untouched, LAS-CLIP seamlessly reverts to vanilla CLIP when no mask is provided, preserving its foundational zero-shot capabilities. # 1 Introduction 

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) [19] has become a foundational building block for vision-language understanding. By training on hundreds of millions of image-text pairs, CLIP acquires a shared embedding space that en-ables strong zero-shot transfer across diverse visual recognition tasks. Subsequent work has scaled this paradigm further through larger datasets [2, 10], improved loss formulations [29], and stronger visual backbones [23], solidifying contrastive vision-language models as a core component of modern visual pipelines. Despite this progress, a fundamental limitation remains: CLIP’s visual en-coder produces a single global representation for the entire image. Many practical arXiv:2610.03370v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 2 Dinh-Duc et al. 

applications, including referring expression comprehension, open-vocabulary de-tection, and subject-driven image generation, require the model to focus on a specific region rather than the whole scene. Bridging this gap between image-level representations and region-level understanding is therefore an important research challenge. Existing approaches to equip CLIP with region awareness can be broadly grouped into three strategies detailed in Section 2.2, each carrying notable draw-backs. For instance, visual prompting [20,26] introduces a distribution gap by al-tering pixel content, input masking [14,22] destroys contextual cues, and encoder modification [24] permanently alters the pre-trained weights while demanding large-scale data and substantial computational resources. In this paper, we propose LAS-CLIP , a Lightweight Adapter Steering ap-proach that adapts CLIP to region-level understanding while keeping every pre-trained parameter frozen. The core idea is a compact MaskAdapter module that takes a binary mask as input and generates per-head, per-layer attention biases. These biases are injected directly into the multi-head self-attention layers of the frozen visual encoder, steering the model’s focus toward the mask-specified region without modifying any original weight. Because the backbone remains un-touched, LAS-CLIP seamlessly reverts to vanilla CLIP behavior when no mask is provided, a property that encoder modification methods cannot offer. An identity regularization term further controls the drift from CLIP’s original rep-resentations. Our contributions are as follows: 

– We introduce LAS-CLIP, a parameter-efficient adapter that steers CLIP’s visual attention toward user-specified regions through learned attention bi-ases, requiring as few as ∼116K trainable parameters while keeping the entire backbone frozen. 

– We design a gated token-mask interaction mechanism that produces atten-tion biases dynamically conditioned on both the spatial mask and current visual tokens. Unlike static or uniform biases that force all attention heads to behave identically, our dynamic gating selectively modulates each head based on its specific semantic role, preserving CLIP’s diverse feature repre-sentations while achieving precise spatial grounding. 

– We demonstrate that LAS-CLIP achieves competitive or superior perfor-mance compared to Alpha-CLIP [24] on zero-shot classification and refer-ring expression comprehension benchmarks, despite using only 100K training samples and two T4 GPUs. Qualitative analysis on attention visualization and subject-driven image generation further confirms that the frozen back-bone retains stronger representational fidelity. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews re-lated work on CLIP adaptation and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Section 3 presents the proposed LAS-CLIP framework, including the MaskAdapter archi-tecture, gated token-mask interaction, training objective, and data preparation pipeline. Section 4 reports experimental results on zero-shot classification, refer-ring expression comprehension, ablation studies, and qualitative analysis. Sec-LAS-CLIP 3

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language Contrastive Pre-training 

CLIP [19] and its successors [2, 10, 23, 29] have established contrastive vision-language pre-training as a foundational paradigm, yet all resulting models pro-duce global image representations without the ability to attend to user-specified regions. 

2.2 Region-Aware CLIP Adaptation 

Adapting CLIP to understand specific image regions has attracted considerable attention. Existing approaches can be broadly grouped into three strategies, namely, visual prompting, input masking, and encoder modification. 

Visual Prompting. Shtedritski et al. [20] showed that drawing a red circle around an object serves as an effective visual prompt for CLIP, and CPT [26] explored a similar strategy using colored fills. These approaches are training-free but modify pixel content, introducing a domain gap with CLIP’s pre-training distribution. FALIP [34] instead inserts foveal attention masks into CLIP’s self-attention, though its bias is uniform across all heads, limiting fine-grained spatial steering. 

Input Masking. MaskAdaptedCLIP [14] fine-tunes CLIP on masked image re-gions with the background replaced by a solid color, but this destroys contex-tual information and degrades classification performance. ReCLIP [22] instead applies Gaussian blurring to the background, preserving more context while still operating at the input level. RegionCLIP [31] pre-trains a region-text alignment objective on top of CLIP features for open-vocabulary detection. 

Encoder Modification. Alpha-CLIP [24] augments CLIP’s patch embedding layer with an auxiliary alpha channel and fine-tunes the visual encoder on millions of RGBA region-text pairs from GRIT [18]. While effective, this approach perma-nently alters the pre-trained weights and demands substantial computational resources. 

2.3 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning 

Adapting large pre-trained models without full fine-tuning has been widely stud-ied through approaches such as bottleneck adapters [8], bias-only tuning [1], and low-rank weight decomposition [9]. In the vision-language domain, prompt-based methods [11, 32, 33] optimize learnable tokens on either the textual or visual branch of CLIP, while feature-level approaches [6, 30] add lightweight residual 4 Dinh-Duc et al. 

layers on top of frozen features. These methods target primarily classification tasks and modify representations in a class-agnostic manner. Notably, none pro-vides a native architectural mechanism to dynamically ingest spatial inputs, such as binary masks, and steer region-specific attention at the level of individ-ual attention heads. This leaves a gap between parameter-efficient adaptation and fine-grained spatial grounding. 

# 3 Method 

This section presents the proposed LAS-CLIP framework. We introduce the nec-essary preliminaries, describe the overall architecture, propose MaskAdapter for generating region-conditioned attention biases, and present the training objec-tive and the data preparation pipeline used to train the lightweight adapter while keeping the CLIP backbone entirely frozen.

As illustrated in Figure 1, given an input image x ∈ R3×H×W and a binary mask 

m ∈ R1×H×W indicating the region of interest, LAS-CLIP produces region-aware visual features while keeping the original CLIP weights entirely frozen. The key idea is to inject a lightweight MaskAdapter module into the last K transformer layers of CLIP’s vision encoder V. This module generates a per-head attention bias matrix Abias ∈ RB×Ha×N ×N , where B is the batch size, Ha is the number of attention heads, and N is the number of visual tokens (including the [CLS] 

token). The bias is added directly to the attention logits QK ⊤ within the frozen multi-head self-attention, steering the model to attend to mask-specified regions without modifying any pre-trained parameters. Identity regularization detailed 6 Dinh-Duc et al. 

in Section 3.4 further controls how far the adapted output may drift from the original representations. In contrast to Alpha-CLIP [24], which fine-tunes the visual encoder on mil-lions of samples from GRIT [18] using multiple A100-80G GPUs, LAS-CLIP is highly parameter-efficient and can be trained in a low-resource setting. Be-cause all original weights remain strictly frozen, the model seamlessly reverts to vanilla CLIP when no mask is provided. This ensures zero degradation of the foundation model’s original capabilities, a critical advantage over methods that permanently alter the backbone’s architecture and weights. Unlike prior approaches that apply a static, uniform bias shared across all at-tention layers and heads [34], LAS-CLIP introduces a dynamic, token-conditioned interaction. In standard Vision Transformers, different attention heads specialize in distinct visual patterns, ranging from local textures to global context. A uni-form bias aggressively overrides this learned diversity. By generating per-head, per-layer biases through a gated mechanism, LAS-CLIP allows each head to inde-pendently decide the degree to which it should attend to the spatial mask, based on the current visual context. This formulation enables fine-grained and flexible steering of attention without collapsing the rich, multi-faceted representations pre-trained within CLIP.

3.5 Data Preparation 

Training LAS-CLIP requires paired image-mask-text data. We adopt the ground-ing data pipeline introduced by Alpha-CLIP [24], which constructs region-text pairs from the GRIT dataset [18]. GRIT provides images annotated with bound-ing boxes and referring expressions. Alpha-CLIP uses the Segment Anything Model, or SAM [12], to convert these bounding boxes into segmentation masks. In our work, we replace SAM with YOLOE [25]. While SAM provides highly precise boundaries, its inference is computationally expensive and excessively slow on standard resource-constrained hardware such as T4 GPUs. By utilizing YOLOE, we establish a highly efficient data pipeline that is designed to be easily scaled up to millions of images in the future with minimal computational cost. More importantly, utilizing YOLOE demonstrates the robustness of LAS-CLIP: we show that our model can effectively learn accurate attention biases even when trained on noisy or imperfect mask annotations, eliminating the strict depen-dency on expensive, pixel-perfect masks. For each annotated bounding box, YOLOE predicts a corresponding segmen-tation mask. A common challenge for any box-prompted segmentation model, including SAM, is that when a bounding box tightly encloses multiple objects, the model may occasionally segment an unintended instance, resulting in a mask that poorly aligns with the target region. To address this general ambiguity, as well as the natural noise in high-speed segmentation pipelines, we apply an IoU-based fallback. Specifically, if the predicted mask has an Intersection-over-Union, or IoU, with the bounding box below a threshold of 0.30, which was empirically chosen based on visual inspection for optimal training stability, we fall back to using the full bounding box region as the mask. Quality Filtering. We apply two filtering criteria to ensure data quality. The mask area ratio relative to the image must lie within [0 .01 , 0.95] , discarding regions that are too small or cover nearly the entire image. In addition, referring expressions shorter than 3 characters are removed. After filtering, we retain 100K high-quality training samples. Further details on the mask generation process and fallback statistics can be found in the supplementary material. 

# 4 Experiments 

To comprehensively validate the effectiveness of our lightweight steering mecha-nism, we evaluate LAS-CLIP across three distinct paradigms of region-aware un-derstanding. We assess zero-shot image classification on ImageNet-S [7] to verify that the model retains robust global semantic recognition. We then evaluate zero-shot referring expression comprehension across three demanding datasets, Ref-COCO [28], RefCOCO+ [28], and RefCOCOg [17], to strictly test fine-grained spatial and relational reasoning. We also demonstrate the plug-and-play gener-ative utility of our frozen encoder in a downstream subject-driven image gen-eration task using BLIP-Diffusion [13]. All models are trained under the low-resource protocol described in Section 3.5. The MaskAdapter is injected into the last K = 3 transformer layers of CLIP’s vision encoder unless stated otherwise. Full training hyperparameters are provided in the supplementary material. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. For zero-shot classification, we adopt the ImageNet-S [7] dataset, which contains 919 classes selected from ImageNet-1k [3] with semantic seg-mentation annotations. The image-level segmentation masks serve as the region input, and the mean of per-class accuracy is used as the evaluation metric. For zero-shot REC, we evaluate on RefCOCO [28], RefCOCO+ [28], and Ref-COCOg [17], which are built on the MS COCO [15] dataset. Following Alpha-CLIP [24], we use object proposals from a pretrained MAttNet [27] detector and obtain masks for each proposal. The original image together with the corre-sponding mask is fed to LAS-CLIP, and the proposal with the highest similarity to the referring expression is selected. We report accuracy using an ensemble of ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 backbones on the standard val and test splits. 

Baselines. We compare LAS-CLIP against the original CLIP [19], MaskAdapt-edCLIP [14], Red Circle [20], and Alpha-CLIP [24]. For the REC task, we ad-ditionally include CPT [26] and ReCLIP [22]. Both ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 backbones are evaluated.

To better understand how each model attends to the specified region, we visual-ize the attention heatmaps using the text-based decomposition method proposed by Gandelsman et al. [5]. This approach decomposes CLIP’s image representa-tion across individual patches, revealing where the model focuses given a text query. Vanilla CLIP takes only the original image as input. For Alpha-CLIP and LAS-CLIP, which receive both the original image and a mask, we evaluate under two conditions: with the correct mask and with an incorrect mask. However, the critical difference emerges under the wrong mask condition. Beyond attention analysis, we evaluate region-aware CLIP variants as vi-sual backbones for a downstream generative task. LAS-CLIP produces outputs that more closely preserve the visual identity of the input subject, maintaining consistent color, texture, and structural charac-teristics. This behavior aligns with the frozen backbone design of LAS-CLIP: because the pre-trained CLIP weights remain unmodified, the encoder retains its original capacity for fine-grained visual representation, and the MaskAdapter steers attention toward the masked region without degrading the underlying fea-ture quality. # LAS-CLIP: Supplementary Material 

# A Implementation Details 

We provide additional implementation details to facilitate reproducibility. 

Architecture. We initialize from the publicly available OpenAI CLIP pretrained weights and evaluate LAS-CLIP on two backbone variants. These variants are ViT-B/16 with D = 768 , Ha = 12 , L = 12 and ViT-L/14 with D = 1024 , Ha = 16 ,

L = 24 . The MaskAdapter uses a hidden dimension of h = 64 for all configura-tions, resulting in only 116,004 trainable parameters for ViT-B/16 and 145,200 for ViT-L/14. Dropout with rate 0.2 is applied in both the mask encoder branch and the gate generator. 

Optimization. We use AdamW [16] with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −3 for the adapter parameters. The logit scale λ is trained with a separate learning rate of 1 × 10 −5. Weight decay is set to 2 × 10 −4. Training runs for 5 epochs with a OneCycleLR [21] schedule and a 10% linear warmup phase. To ensure stable contrastive learning, we apply 16 gradient accumulation steps to a base batch size of 128, yielding an effective batch size of 2048. Gradient clipping is applied at ∥g∥max = 1 .0.

Training Cost. Thanks to the compact adapter design and the small number of trainable parameters, the entire training procedure is highly efficient. On two NVIDIA T4 GPUs, training the ViT-B/16 variant completes in approximately 2 hours, while the larger ViT-L/14 variant requires roughly 6 hours. This low com-putational overhead makes LAS-CLIP straightforward to reproduce on widely accessible, consumer-grade hardware without requiring high-end GPU clusters. 

Loss Configuration. The identity regularization weight is α = 0 .5. The full-image augmentation probability is paug = 0 .1.

Mask Generation Details. We specifically use the YOLOE-26x-seg model for mask prediction. During this process, 12,995 out of the 100,000 training samples fell back to the bounding box mask due to the 0.30 IoU threshold, successfully filtering out ambiguous box-prompt cases to ensure optimal training stability as discussed in Section 3.5 of the main paper. 

Zero-shot Classification Protocol. We follow the standard CLIP zero-shot eval-uation protocol using the 80 prompt templates provided by Radford et al. [19]. For each class, text features are computed for all 80 templates and averaged to produce a single class embedding. The cosine similarity between the image feature and each averaged class embedding determines the prediction. 16 Dinh-Duc et al. 

Zero-shot REC Evaluation Protocol. Following Alpha-CLIP [24], we use object proposals predicted by a pretrained MAttNet [27] detector. For each proposal, a segmentation mask is obtained using SAM [12] with the proposal bounding box as the box prompt. The original image and the corresponding mask are fed to LAS-CLIP to produce a region-aware image feature. We also apply Gaussian blurring with standard deviation σ = 100 to the original image and compute a second feature from the blurred input paired with the same mask, following the preprocessing protocol of ReCLIP [22] and Alpha-CLIP [24]. The similarity scores from the original and blurred inputs are summed for each proposal, and the proposal with the highest total cosine similarity to the referring expression text feature is selected as the prediction. For the backbone ensemble reported in the main paper, we compute similarity scores independently from both ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 backbones. The final score for each proposal is the sum of scores across both backbones, and the proposal with the highest combined score is selected.

# D Mask Robustness Analysis 

To quantitatively evaluate robustness to imperfect masks, we systematically per-turb the ground-truth segmentation masks on ImageNet-S using three strate-gies. These strategies include erosion for under-segmentation, dilation for over-segmentation, and spatial shifts. Each perturbation is calibrated to produce masks at approximate IoU levels of 0.90, 0.70, 0.50, and 0.30 relative to the ground truth. All experiments use the ViT-L/14 backbone. Figure S3 reports the results. Under erosion, both methods degrade compa-rably. Under dilation and spatial shifts, LAS-CLIP exhibits substantially smaller accuracy drops than Alpha-CLIP [24], especially at lower IoU levels. At IoU ≈ 0.30, the gap reaches 2.0% for dilation and 1.7% for spatial shifts. These results in-dicate that LAS-CLIP’s token-conditioned gating mechanism mitigates over-reliance on the spatial mask prior, consistent with the qualitative attention anal-ysis in the main paper.
