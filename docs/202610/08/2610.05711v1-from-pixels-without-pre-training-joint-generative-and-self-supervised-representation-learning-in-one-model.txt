Abstract
Strong image generation models today are conditioned on class labels, aligned to
frozen pretrained encoders, or built on separately trained autoencoders or tokenizers.
While effective, the cost is that generation becomes contingent on supervision and
pretraining: labels must be annotated, and encoders, autoencoders, and tokenizers
must be pretrained for the target domain. This paper studies joint generative and
self-supervised representation learning in one model, such that generation is
self-conditioned without any labels or pretrained models. This is challenging since
the two objectives are mismatched: contrastive learning consumes clean augmented
views and keeps coarse, invariant semantics, while flow matching consumes noisy
images and must preserve the fine detail and spatial layout that contrastive learning
discards. We propose a framework Self-conditioned Generation on Self-supervised
representation (SCION), whose core is a single pixel-space encoder conditioned
on both the diffusion timestep and a conditioning embedding. The encoder serves
two roles that differ in what that embedding is. For representation learning, the
conditioning embedding is one learned global vector shared by all images, and the
encoder’s [CLS] token yields the semantic embedding trained by the contrastive
loss. For generative training, the conditioning embedding is the [CLS] embedding
of the image itself, and the encoder’s patch tokens pass through a decoder to predict
the image. To sample without a reference image at inference, we also learn a
prior over the embedding in the same run. With gradient-norm balancing and stop-
gradient tricks, all losses are jointly optimized in a single run. The resulting method
is fully self-supervised, with no pretrained model and no labels. On ImageNet
256 × 256, with the same JiT-B/16 recipe and without representation guidance,
SCION reaches 8.92 FID, surpassing the class-unconditional baselines iREPA,
which aligns to a pretrained DINOv2 (46.44), and RCG, which conditions on it
(14.27). Under the JiT-L/16 recipe, SCION achieves the best pixel-space class-
unconditional FID of 5.89 and 3.47 without and with representation guidance,
outperforming RCG with the ADM recipe (6.24).

Introduction
Diffusion and flow matching models have achieved remarkable success in image generation (Ho
et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Yet many strong systems rely on
class labels for semantic conditioning (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Li & He, 2026), frozen pretrained
encoders for conditioning or representation alignment (Li et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2025; Singh et al.,

dependencies add annotation or pretraining costs and complicate extension to domains where suitable
labels or pretrained models are unavailable. A central challenge is therefore to enable semantically
self-conditioned generation, where the semantic representations that condition generation are learned
within the diffusion model itself, jointly with the generative process.
We pursue semantic self-conditioned generation under four “S” requirements: self-contained, without
pretrained components, including autoencoders and tokenizers; self-supervised, without label supervi-
sion anywhere in the pipeline, including that inherited through pretrained components; single-model,
with representations produced by the generator’s own network; and single-stage, with all components
learned jointly from scratch. Existing methods (cf. Table 1) address different aspects of this goal
but satisfy these requirements only partially (Li & He, 2026; Yu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024a, 2023;
Preechakul et al., 2022; Hudson et al., 2024; Wang & He, 2025). These comparisons highlight the
remaining challenge: achieving semantic self-conditioning under all four “S” requirements, with
useful representations and unconditional synthesis learned together within one model.

A natural approach to semantically self-conditioned
generation is to learn the conditioning representations
through contrastive self-supervised learning, which ex-
tracts semantics directly from unlabeled images (Chen
et al., 2020a; He et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2022). How-
ever, integrating self-supervised representation learn-
ing with flow matching in one network requires recon-
ciling their conflicting input and output requirements.
Contrastive learning operates on clean augmented
views and encourages compact semantic embeddings
that suppress variations in appearance and spatial lay-
out. Flow matching instead operates on noisy images
and requires dense predictions that recover fine visual
detail and spatial structure. The shared network must
therefore accommodate both clean and noisy inputs
while supporting semantic invariance without discard-
ing information needed for synthesis. Joint training
further couples the generator to an evolving condition-
ing representation, and sampling requires obtaining
conditioning embeddings without a reference image.
To address these challenges, we propose Self-
Conditioned Generation on Self-Supervised Representation (SCION), a framework for semantically
self-conditioned generation through joint generative and self-supervised representation learning. At
its core is a single pixel-space transformer encoder conditioned on both a timestep and a conditioning
embedding. Our key idea is to use this encoder in two conditioning modes with different readouts.
For representation learning, the encoder processes clean augmented views using a learned global
conditioning vector shared across all images, and a contrastive objective trains its [CLS] embedding.
For generative learning, the same encoder processes noisy images conditioned on the clean image’s
own [CLS] embedding, and a decoder maps its patch tokens to a clean-image prediction under a
flow matching objective. This design keeps contrastive inputs clean while using spatial features for
generation, allowing both objectives to train the shared encoder.
To coordinate joint optimization, we employ gradient-norm balancing and stop-gradient operations.
We also train a prior over the learned embeddings in the same run, enabling generation without
reference images. Randomly withholding the conditioning embedding during training also supports
classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021), with guidance supplied by learned representations
rather than class labels. All components are trained jointly from scratch, without labels, pretrained
teachers, latent autoencoders, or tokenizers. We evaluate on ImageNet at 256×256 against flow-based
baselines matched on the JiT-B/16 recipe. SCION reaches 8.92 FID, improving by 6.1× over the
strongest self-contained baseline (54.14), and outperforming methods that import pretrained seman-
tics, either aligning with them (iREPA, 46.44) or conditioning on them (RCG at matched training
budget, 14.27). Self-representation guidance further reduces SCION to 4.20 FID, outperforming
representation-guided RCG (5.01) without importing a pretrained encoder. At larger scale, SCION
(JiT-L/16) reaches 5.89 unguided FID, improving on the 6.24 reported by pixel-space RCG with an

2.1
Flow matching
Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023) is a generative
modeling framework that learns a continuous-time vector field whose integral curves transport a
noise distribution pnoise to the data distribution pdata. Its trajectories follow the ordinary differential
equation (ODE) dxt/dt = vt(xt) started at x0 = ϵ ∼pnoise, so that x1 ∼pdata: the velocity field
is the time derivative of this marginal path, and solving the ODE is what generates samples.
The marginal field is defined from a conditional path between a noise sample and a data sample,
which we denote by ext. Most works choose the conditional path according to the linear interpolant:

Contrastive Self-supervised Representation Learning
For self-supervised representation learning, we consider contrastive objectives, which learn a repre-
sentation by pulling together strong augmented views of the same image and pushing apart views of
different images (Chen et al., 2020a; He et al., 2020). Thus, for a given image xi, we take its two
views Aa(xi) and Ab(xi) as a positive pair, and define its negatives as the set of augmented views of
all other images in the dataset, S−= {A(x) : x ∈D \ {xi}, A ∼A}.

head that maps the representation to a space where the contrastive loss is applied. We denote by
E(x) = hψ(fθ(x)) the representation for contrastive learning.
Conventional approaches of contrastive learning either require a large batch size (Chen et al., 2020a)
or maintain a large memory bank (He et al., 2020). To avoid these, we follow the recent advances in
optimizing a global contrastive objective (Yuan et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2024):

3
Self-conditioned Generation on Self-Supervised Representation
In this section, we present the proposed framework SCION, including the model structure and the
training objective and strategies.
3.1
The Unified Model
Since we model images directly in pixel space, we adopt the “Just Image Transformer” (JiT) archi-
tecture for image generation (Li & He, 2026). To integrate generation with representation learning,
we partition JiT into two components: an encoder fθ and a decoder gϕ, parameterized by θ and ϕ,
respectively. Both fθ and gϕ consist of multiple Transformer blocks. To process an image, we split it
into non-overlapping patches, linearly embed the patches into a sequence of T tokens, and prepend a
learnable [CLS] token.
One encoder, two purposes. The encoder processes two types of inputs for two distinct purposes.
For flow matching, it encodes a noise-diffused image ext = tx + (1 −t)ϵ. For contrastive learning,
it encodes a strongly augmented, non-diffused image A(x). To accommodate both types of inputs,
we condition the encoder fθ on the timestep t ∈[0, 1], where t = 1 corresponds to a non-diffused
image. To enable semantically self-conditioned generation, we additionally condition the encoder on
an embedding vector c, whose construction is described below. We therefore write the encoder as
fθ(z | t, c), where z denotes either a clean image x, its augmented image A(x) or a noise-diffused
image ext.
The encoder output also serves two different purposes. For contrastive learning, it should provide a
compact semantic representation that is invariant to variations in appearance and spatial layout. For
generation, it must preserve fine-grained visual details and spatial structure. To accommodate these
different requirements within a single encoder, we use two readouts from its output: the [CLS] token
embedding e ∈Rd used for representation learning, and the patch-token embeddings h ∈RT ×d
used for generation. For convenience, we introduce two readout operators, πe and πh, which extract
the [CLS] token embedding and the patch-token embeddings from fθ(z | t, c), respectively.
Two conditioning modes for two purposes. For generative modeling, we would like the encoder
to process the noise-diffused image ext conditioned on a semantic representation e(x) of the corre-
sponding clean image, namely, fθ(ext | t, e(x)). However, the representation e(x) must itself be
produced by the same encoder, creating an apparent circular dependency: the representation used to
condition the encoder cannot simultaneously depend on that conditioned forward pass.

To break this dependency, we introduce a learnable null embedding e∅. The semantic representation
of a clean image is first obtained through a null conditioned encoding step, e(x) = πe ◦fθ(x | 1, e∅).
Likewise, for a strongly augmented view A(x), its representation for contrastive learning is computed
as e(A(x)) = πe ◦fθ(A(x) | 1, e∅). The resulting clean-image representation e(x) is then used as
the semantic conditioning signal when processing the corresponding noise-diffused image, yielding
ht = πh ◦fθ(ext | t, e(x)). Thus, the same encoder operates in two conditioning modes: null-
conditioned encoding for representation learning and semantic-conditioned encoding for generation.
The decoder.
Given a noise-diffused image ext, the decoder takes the encoder’s patch-token
embeddings ht = πh ◦fθ(ext | t, e(x)) as input. As with the encoder, we condition the decoder on
both the timestep t and the clean-image representation e(x). The prediction is therefore given by
gϕ(ht | t, e(x)).
In summary, the unified model uses the same encoder in two complementary ways. For representation
learning, an augmented image is encoded with null conditioning as e(A(x)) = πe◦fθ(A(x) | 1, e∅),
and its [CLS] token embedding is used for contrastive learning. For generation, the clean-image
representation e(x) = πe ◦fθ(x | 1, e∅) is used to condition both the encoder and decoder when
processing ext, with ht = πh ◦fθ(ext | t, e(x)) and prediction gϕ(ht | t, e(x)). In this way, a single
encoder simultaneously supports semantic representation learning and semantically conditioned
image generation. To implement conditioning in both the encoder and decoder, we adopt adaLN-
Zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023). The proposed model structure of SCION is presented in Figure 2.

3.3
Training Strategies and Sampling
To facilitate the training of the unified model by optimizing the joint objective, we employ the
following two strategies.
6


Stop Gradients. For CFM, the gradient equivalence between conditional and marginal velocity
regression established by Lipman et al. (2023) relies on target probability paths and velocity fields
that are independent of the learnable parameters. In our setting, the conditioning representation e(x)
depends on θ, so differentiating through it introduces an additional gradient path that is not covered
by the standard equivalence argument (Appendix D). We therefore apply a stop-gradient operation to
e(x) in the CFM loss, treating it as fixed conditioning during each update. We likewise detach e(x)
in Lprior(ω; x) and LCBC(θ; x). Consequently, only the contrastive loss backpropagates through the
computation of e(x), while the CFM and CBC losses update the shared encoder through the fθ.
Gradient Balancing. The losses LCFM and LGCL operate on different scales: the former is a
weighted squared error in pixel space, whereas the latter is a contrastive objective governed by
the temperature τ. Consequently, the trade-off coefficient λ has no natural a priori scale. In our
measurements, their gradient norms at the shared encoder differ by more than an order of magnitude,
with ∥∇LCL∥≈40 ∥∇LCFM∥. This ratio also varies with augmentation strength, learning rate, and
batch size, making a fixed λ difficult to transfer across settings. We therefore adapt λ to balance the
weighted gradient norms at a subset of shared encoder parameters ς ⊆θ:

Sampling at Inference.
At inference time, we first generate a standardized embedding by inte-
grating the representation prior’s ODE, defined by the model eω, starting from embedding-space
noise ϵ′. We then reverse the standardization to obtain the representation e and use it to condition the
image-generation ODE, defined by the model Fθ,ϕ = gϕ ◦πh ◦fθ (see Figure 3(b)).
Representation Guidance.
To enable classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021) with rep-
resentation conditioning at inference, we replace the conditioning representation with a second
learned null embedding c∅with probability 0.1 during training. This allows the same network
to learn both representation-conditioned and unconditional generation. At inference time, we in-
tegrate the image-generation ODE using the guided velocity field. Let the predicted velocity be
vθ,ϕ(xt, t | c) = (Fθ,ϕ(xt, t | c) −xt)/(1 −t):

SCION to identify the contribution of each component and compare its representation with that of a
contrastive-only model.

Setup. All controlled methods use the same JiT-B/16 backbone (Li & He, 2026) with attention
residuals (Appendix F), flow matching recipe, ImageNet-1K (256 × 256) training data (Deng et al.,
2009; Russakovsky et al., 2015), and a total training budget of 400 epochs. The baselines that import
semantics use a frozen DINOv2 ViT-B encoder (Oquab et al., 2024), pretrained on external data.
REPA and iREPA retain their original split of 4 encoder and 8 decoder blocks, whereas Dispersive
Loss and SCION use 8 encoder and 4 decoder blocks. We split RCG’s training budget between its
generator and prior, training for 200 epochs each. We also evaluate RCG with both its original prior
architecture and ours: the original architecture performs better, so we report that result.
Evaluation. We evaluate generation with FID and Inception Score (IS) under the ADM protocol
(Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) on 50K samples. We evaluate representations using ImageNet top-1
accuracy (Acc.) and the average accuracy across eight datasets for transfer learning (transfer-8;
datasets listed in Appendix E.2). For both probes, we train a logistic-regression classifier on the
frozen representations produced by the model. For the baselines, the representation is the mean
pooling of patch token embeddings at the encoder–decoder split, or after the last block for vanilla JiT
and RCG; for SCION, we use its [CLS] token at its own split. Training and evaluation details are in
Appendix E, and architecture details in Appendix F.
4.1
Controlled matched-recipe comparison
Table 2 compares all methods under the matched recipe. SCION achieves the best performance
on both generation and representation capabilities. On unconditional generation, it achieves 8.92
FID, greatly improving on the best self-contained baseline (54.14). It also improves on the strongest
alignment baseline, iREPA (46.44), and on RCG (14.27), both of which import semantics from a
pretrained DINOv2 encoder. Moreover, SCION surpasses the class-conditional baselines, whose best
unguided FID is 24.93, without using labels or an external encoder. Notably, after only 200 epochs,
SCION already leads every matched baseline and surpasses RCG’s 400-epoch-equivalent checkpoint
on all four metrics, despite RCG’s additional frozen prior-training stage (Table 9, Appendix C.2).
External DINOv2 semantics improve the other baselines in different ways: iREPA transfers part of
them into the generator’s own features, reaching 65.11% Acc. at 46.44 FID, whereas RCG improves
generation to 14.27 FID but reaches only 6.54% Acc. and 30.28% transfer-8. SCION achieves
72.35% Acc. and 79.89% transfer-8, exceeding every baseline. Together with Table 1, these results
show that SCION is the only controlled method satisfying all four “S” requirements while also
achieving the best generation and representation results.

4.3
Ablations
To isolate the contribution of each component, we use the same JiT-B/16 recipe as the controlled
comparison and train every model for 200 epochs.
From flow matching to SCION. We decompose SCION by sequentially adding its components to
unconditional flow matching. Table 5 shows that contrastive learning first makes the representation
useful, increasing Acc. from 10.65% to 65.43% and transfer-8 from 26.19% to 76.69%, while slightly
improving FID from 61.17 to 57.40. Gradient-norm balancing further reduces FID to 54.79 while
maintaining representation quality and avoiding manual selection of the contrastive weight. Adding
representation self-conditioning with the jointly learned prior converts these representation gains into
substantially better generation, reducing FID to 11.66. Finally, CBC completes SCION and reaches
10.10 FID, with 71.32% Acc. and 79.73% transfer-8. Appendix C.1 provides a more detailed ladder.
Comparison with contrastive-only training. Table 5 also compares with a contrastive-only control
using the same 8-layer encoder, evaluated at 200 epochs. Its learning rate, temperature, and weight
decay are tuned independently for contrastive learning. SCION reaches 71.32% Acc., close to the
control’s 71.76%, while attaining higher transfer-8 accuracy (79.73% versus 77.68%). Thus, joint
training retains representation quality comparable to dedicated contrastive learning while also learning
to generate. Appendix C.1 repeats this comparison at 200, 400, and 600 epochs.

tions from real-image representations and interpolations between them. Appendix C.4 shows that
conditioning on the prior instead of the encoder’s representations raises precision and lowers recall.

5
Conclusion
We presented SCION, a framework that jointly learns image generation and semantic representations
in a single pixel-space model, trained from scratch without labels or pretrained components. By
integrating contrastive learning with flow matching, representation self-conditioning, and a jointly
learned embedding prior, SCION supports both representation extraction and unconditional image
generation. Experiments on ImageNet demonstrate substantial improvements over matched generative
baselines while retaining representation quality competitive with dedicated contrastive learning.
These results show that strong generation and useful representations can be learned together in a
self-contained, self-supervised training process. Future work will explore downstream applications
and extensions to domains where labels and pretrained models are scarce.
Ethics statement
Like other image-generation methods, SCION could be used to produce misleading or harmful
imagery. We consider the potential for misuse of the models studied here to be limited by their current
image fidelity, 256 × 256 resolution, and class-unconditional sampling without text-based or explicit
identity control. These limitations do not eliminate risk: generated images and learned representations
may reflect biases or sensitive content in the training data, and we have not evaluated training-image
memorization or demographic fairness. By removing the need for labels and pretrained components,
the method also lowers the barrier to training on new domains and could enable more capable models.
Such extensions would require assessment of risks appropriate to their capabilities and intended use.
Training uses multiple NVIDIA H200 GPUs (Appendix E), with an associated energy cost. Our
single-stage approach avoids separate pretraining of encoders, autoencoders, or tokenizers, although
we do not establish a reduction in total compute or energy consumption relative to other approaches.

A
Extended related work
Semantic conditioning for generation. Conditional GANs established that class information can
substantially improve image synthesis (Mirza & Osindero, 2014; Brock et al., 2019). Thus, although
DDPM initially demonstrated high-quality unconditional synthesis (Ho et al., 2020), it was soon
followed by Improved DDPM incorporating class labels into diffusion (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021).
Classifier guidance further strengthened the effect of this semantic signal (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021),
while classifier-free guidance subsequently removed the need for a separate classifier, retaining label
conditioning (Ho & Salimans, 2021). Together, these developments made semantics a central lever
for generation, but supplied them directly through annotations.
Alongside this use of labels, efforts to reduce computation brought another source of semantics into
the pipeline. Latent diffusion and flow models generate through pretrained autoencoders (Rombach
et al., 2022; Dao et al., 2023), a setting later continued by transformer generators such as DiT and SiT
(Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024). Although introduced for compression, these autoencoders can
inherit semantic information from their training objectives. VQGAN (Esser et al., 2021), for example,
uses a perceptual loss (Zhang et al., 2018) based on supervised features (Simonyan & Zisserman,
2014). The semantic information carried by these tokenizers can therefore shape the representations
learned on top of them (Chen et al., 2025b) and also benefit generation beyond reconstruction fidelity
(Wang et al., 2024).
Importing representations for generation. Beyond the semantics inherited from autoencoders,
generators can also draw on representations supplied explicitly by pretrained encoders. Some align
autoencoder latents with frozen features (Yao et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025), while representation
autoencoders generate directly in a pretrained encoder’s latent space (Zheng et al., 2025). Others
bring the same semantic signal into the generator itself through feature alignment, as in REPA and
iREPA (Yu et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2026). Since fixed alignment can conflict with generative
detail, subsequent work refines how pretrained features interact with generative training (Wang et al.,
2025b; Wu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). ReDi instead models images and pretrained features jointly
(Kouzelis et al., 2025).
Beyond defining the latent space or guiding internal features, representations can serve directly
as conditions. RCG and DLC take this route, conditioning on pretrained embeddings or discrete
codes derived from them, and training separate models to sample them (Li et al., 2024a; Lavoie
et al., 2025). Related retrieval and instance-conditioned methods obtain conditions from real images
(Blattmann et al., 2022; Bordes et al., 2022; Casanova et al., 2021), while earlier approaches replace
labels with clusters or proxy labels (Lucic et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Bao et al., 2022a; Hu
et al., 2023). These approaches show that semantic conditioning need not rely on class labels, but
importing representations introduces dependencies on pretrained components or separate training
stages. Learning these representations within the generator itself would therefore reduce the pipeline’s
external dependencies.
Self-contained generation. The pretrained components used by many of these approaches motivate
a complementary direction that removes such dependencies. Pixel-space diffusion and flow models
avoid the upstream autoencoder (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2023), and
recent recipes have made this setting increasingly competitive at high resolution (Hoogeboom et al.,
2023, 2025; Chen et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025a; Li & He, 2026). Operating on pixels therefore
removes one dependency, although it does not itself replace the semantic signal supplied by class
labels. Dispersive Loss and Repulsor extend this effort to representation regularization, pushing
apart intermediate features without a pretrained representation encoder (Wang & He, 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025). In particular, Dispersive Loss finds positive-pair regularization sensitive to noisy-
view construction and therefore retains only contrastive repulsion, arguing that denoising supplies
attraction. The features nevertheless remain auxiliary regularizers, without downstream representation
evaluation or explicit semantic conditioning, and the high-quality ImageNet settings retain class
labels.
Joint generation and representation learning. Self-supervised learning offers a source of such
semantics through pretext tasks (Noroozi & Favaro, 2016; Gidaris et al., 2018), masked modeling
(Bao et al., 2022b; He et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2021), self-distillation (Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al.,
2021), and contrastive objectives (van den Oord et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2020a; He et al., 2020). The
latter learn invariances by matching augmented views of the same image while separating different

A different route makes the learned representation a condition for a diffusion decoder. DiffAE learns
this code through reconstruction, then fits a latent prior for unconditional sampling (Preechakul
et al., 2022). InfoDiffusion likewise conditions diffusion on learned codes, sampled from a prior or a
separately trained prior model (Wang et al., 2023). SODA moves from reconstruction to cross-view
generation, using a source view’s code to generate a related view (Hudson et al., 2024). All three
methods produce their representations with an encoder separate from the diffusion decoder, and
SODA additionally requires a source image for synthesis. SCION follows the joint learning goal
while bringing the representation into the pixel-space generator itself, where it is learned, evaluated,
and used to condition synthesis. A prior trained in the same run supplies conditions without a source
image, combining the four requirements summarized in Table 6 and defined in Section 1.

Additional Experimental Results
C.1
Detailed ablations
This section expands the construction ablation in Section 4.3. Table 7 decomposes our joint-training
recipe. It first isolates adding the contrastive loss with different view splits to the flow matching
model. It then cumulatively adds gradient-norm balancing, representation self-conditioning with the
learned prior, and, lastly, the CBC loss. All results are reported using the JiT-B/16 recipe, trained
for 200 epochs in the unconditional setting. Table 8 then tracks SCION and capacity-matched
contrastive-only controls over training. All rows train the same 8-layer encoder from scratch, while
the controls use learning rate, temperature, and weight decay tuned independently for contrastive
learning. Each control is a single 600-epoch run with a cosine learning-rate schedule, evaluated at its
200-, 400-, and 600-epoch checkpoints.
Contrastive learning benefits both tasks before self-conditioning.
Adding contrastive learning
with one original and two augmented inputs substantially improves representation quality while
modestly improving the still weak pre-prior generation baseline. FID decreases from 61.17 to
57.40, while Acc. rises from 10.65% to 65.43% and transfer-8 from 26.19% to 76.69%. Using only
augmented inputs obtains similar representation quality but degrades FID to 70.46. From rung 2 on,
the representation is read from a [CLS] token. With it, gradient-norm balancing reaches 54.79 FID,
better than every fixed contrastive weight in rung 2 (56.00 to 60.25), without materially changing
representation quality and without manual selection of the contrastive weight.
Self-conditioning is the generative lever.
Adding representation self-conditioning together with
the learned prior converts the improved representation into substantially better generation, reducing
FID to 11.66 while improving Acc. and transfer-8 to 70.97% and 78.86%, respectively. Adding CBC
completes SCION and further improves generation, reducing FID to 10.10.
Comparison with contrastive-only training.
The control with one original and two augmented
views achieves the highest Acc. at every budget, and the control with two augmented views also
exceeds SCION at 400 and 600 epochs, whereas SCION achieves the highest transfer-8 score at
every budget. Therefore, across the 200, 400, and 600-epoch budgets, SCION learns representations
broadly comparable to those from dedicated contrastive training.
C.2
Controlled comparison at a shorter training budget
Table 9 repeats the matched comparison after 200 epochs. SCION already leads every single-run
baseline on both generation and representation metrics, and exceeds RCG’s 400-epoch-equivalent
checkpoint on every reported metric.
C.3
Unconditional generation system-level comparison
Table 10 presents latent and pixel-space published unconditional ImageNet systems in a common
dependency view. The lowest latent-space FIDs come from multi-stage pipelines built on pretrained
autoencoders or tokenizers and, for ReDi and DLC, external DINOv2 features trained beyond
ImageNet. Within the pixel-space block, SCION (L/16) reaches 5.89 FID without an autoencoder,
tokenizer, external model, labels, or external data, and representation guidance reduces this to 3.47
using a prior learned in the same run. Since architectures, protocols, and training budgets differ across
systems, the table should be read as a system-level comparison. Tables 2 and 4 give the controlled
matched-recipe comparisons.
C.4
Analysis of the representation prior
At inference, the generator is conditioned on a representation sampled from the prior eω, so sample
quality depends on both the generator and the prior. To separate the two, we replace the prior sample
with the representation e(x) of a real training image, which we call oracle conditioning. It requires
real images at generation time, so it serves as a diagnostic rather than as a way to sample.

Protocol.
All results use SCION (B/16) at 400 epochs, without guidance. We draw a conditioning
set of 50 images per class, 50K in total, from the ImageNet training set with a fixed seed, and generate
one image from the representation of each. The prior arm consists of 50K images generated by the
same model from prior samples, namely the samples behind the 8.92 FID in Table 2. We compute
precision and recall (Appendix E.2) of both arms against two references: the conditioning set itself,
and a disjoint set drawn in the same way from the remaining training images. For each reference we
use a random 10K subset for precision and recall computation, matching what ADM does.
Real representations raise recall, prior samples raise precision.
Table 11 reports the results. The
prior arm serves as a control: its samples were generated from neither reference, and it scores nearly
the same against both, with precision .718 and .722 and recall .524 and .510. The two references
therefore behave alike for samples not generated from either, so a gap between them in the oracle
arm reflects that its samples were generated from the conditioning images.
The oracle arm shows such a gap. Its recall is .828 against the conditioning set and .664 against the
disjoint set, so the generated images cover the specific images they were conditioned on better than
other images from the same distribution. The representation thus carries image-specific content into
the generated image.

E
Experiment details
This appendix gives the training and evaluation setup behind Section 4. Table 12 summarizes the
main hyperparameters at both scales. Unless an experiment states otherwise, the settings below are
the defaults for every ImageNet-1K (Deng et al., 2009; Russakovsky et al., 2015) run.
All experiments are run on nodes of 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs. To speed up experiments, we train on
up to 3 nodes for B/16 and 6 nodes for L/16.
E.1
Training setup
We follow JiT (Li & He, 2026) for the generative hyperparameters and for LCFM, including the
optimizer, the augmentation policy of the original image and the flow matching loss. We depart from
it in two places, both chosen once and applied to every image generator in the comparison, baselines
included: the base learning rate, which is tuned to 1 · 10−4; and the attention residuals of Appendix F.
Since every arm trains at a global batch of 1024, the learning rate is 4 · 10−4. For the contrastive
hyperparameters and the augmentation policy of the augmented images we follow SogCLR (Yuan
et al., 2022).
The RCG baseline is our own re-implementation on this backbone and conditions on DINOv2 (Oquab
et al., 2024) embeddings. For its prior, we use the architecture of their official implementation2, a
residual MLP with 12 blocks of width 1536, trained for 200 epochs with our flow-matching objective
and prior timestep schedule. For a fair comparison, we also ran RCG with our prior, which performed
worse.
Augmentations.
We use kO = 1 original image and kA = 2 augmented images. The original
image follows JiT’s preprocessing, a center crop to the training resolution and a random horizontal
flip with probability 0.5. It is the only input the generative path sees and it is also one contrastive
anchor. The two augmented images are used by the representation path alone and follow the SimCLR
policy (Chen et al., 2020a; Grill et al., 2020): random resized crop with scale in [0.08, 1.0], color
jitter of strength (0.4, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1) applied with probability 0.8, random grayscale with probability
0.2, Gaussian blur with σ uniform in [0.1, 2.0], and a random horizontal flip. This is what exposes the
shared encoder to both regimes every step, noised original images for generation and clean augmented
ones for the invariances.
2https://github.com/LTH14/rcg
28


Timestep sampling.
For both flow matching losses we sample t from the logit-normal schedule
(Esser et al., 2024): draw s ∼N(µ, σ2) and set t = sigmoid(s), which we write logit(t) ∼
N(µ, σ2). The image path uses JiT’s default (µ, σ) = (−0.8, 0.8), and the prior path (µ′, σ′) =
(−1.2, 0.6), the latter selected on a frozen-prior grid and applied to the RCG baseline as well.
Losses.
The contrastive objective is the multi-view form of the Global Contrastive Loss (Yuan
et al., 2022), with fixed temperature τ = 0.15, moving-average parameter γ = 0.9, and negatives
gathered across GPUs. The generative objective is the conditional flow matching loss (Lipman et al.,
2023) with x-prediction and the velocity parameterization of JiT (Li & He, 2026). We clamp the
1 −t denominator at max(1 −t, 0.05) for numerical stability near t = 1. The weight λ on LCL
comes from the balancer of Section 3.3 and Algorithm 2: gradient norms are read on the feed-forward
submodule of the last encoder block, the estimate is refreshed every 10 steps, and the two moving
averages use a decay ramped linearly from 0.9 to 0.99 over the first 5000 steps. Their ratio is clamped
to [10−3, 1] and multiplied by λ0 = 1. The CBC weight is fixed at η = 0.01 and is not balanced.
None of these constants are tuned.
Optimization.
At B/16 we train with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95),
in bfloat16 mixed precision, under a constant learning rate with a linear warmup over 5 epochs and
weight decay 0. At L/16 the parameters split across two optimizers: Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) takes
the two-dimensional parameters inside the transformer blocks, at learning rate 2 · 10−2 and weight
decay 5 · 10−4, and everything else stays on AdamW at the same learning rate and weight decay as at
B/16. Moreover, for L/16, we use a BATCHNORM (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) after the [CLS] token at
the encoder-decoder split. We keep two exponential moving averages of the weights, with decays
0.9999 and 0.9996.
E.2
Evaluation
Checkpoint selection.
All metrics are computed on EMA weights. Section 4.1 reports the 400-
epoch checkpoint, the budget at which every baseline stops, Section 4.3 reports 200 epochs, and
Section 4.2 reports SCION (B/16) at 800 epochs and SCION (L/16) at 400. We evaluate the EMA
with decay 0.9999, except for the system-level comparison, where we evaluate both exponential
moving averages and select the 0.9996 one only for SCION (L/16).
Generation.
All generation numbers use 50K samples drawn with a Heun ODE solver (Heun,
1900) with 50 steps (49 Heun steps and a final Euler step). For the class-conditional baselines the
batch is 50 samples for each of the 1000 ImageNet classes. SCION is class-unconditional and does
not use labels for generation either. Instead, we unconditionally sample from the learned prior eω
the conditioning vectors for the generator. Thus, its batch carries no class structure by construction.
Where guidance is reported, it is applied on the interval t ∈[0.1, 1.0] (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024) at
a scale swept from 1.0 in steps of 0.1 on 8000 samples, continuing while FID keeps improving.
Generation metrics.
We evaluate generation with the ADM suite (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021),
using their reference batches and official implementation.3 All metrics are computed from Inception-
V3 outputs on the 50K generated images; all but the Inception Score also read the suite’s ImageNet

Both probes freeze the network and read one vector per image. For SCION that

Architecture details
SCION is a JiT-style pixel-space transformer (Li & He, 2026) with three changes. First, we split the
transformer depth into a shared encoder fθ and a generative decoder gϕ, two contiguous portions
of one ViT-style backbone (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) with total depth L = Lenc + Ldec. Second,
we prepend a learnable [CLS] token and read it out at the encoder/decoder boundary as the global
embedding e. Third, we attach a contrastive projection head hψ to that embedding while the patch
tokens continue through the decoder for flow matching. Additionally, we condition the generative
path on sg(e) and learn a prior eω (Li et al., 2024a) over these embeddings for class-unconditional
sampling. Table 13 lists each component with its input and output spaces.
All remaining architectural choices follow JiT, except for the attention residuals below, which every
model we train uses. For completeness, we go into each component in the following paragraphs.
Transformer block.
Each block uses pre-RMSNORM (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019), multi-head
self-attention with QK-norm (Henry et al., 2020) and two-dimensional rotary positional embeddings
(Su et al., 2024), then a second pre-RMSNORM and a SWIGLU feed-forward network (Shazeer,
2020) whose inner width is 8
3d, an MLP ratio of 4 under SWIGLU’s two-thirds rescaling. As in
DiT and JiT, both residual branches are modulated by adaLN-Zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023): a linear
projection of the conditioning vector produces shift, scale and gate parameters for the two sublayers,
and the modulation layers are zero-initialized so each branch begins near the identity.

reading the running residual sum, a sublayer reads a learned softmax-weighted mixture over its depth
history and appends its own output to that history: the keys are the normalized history entries, and the
query is a learned vector per token role (patch, [CLS] or in-context tokens). Writing s1, . . . , sn ∈Rd

Patch and positional embeddings.
Images are tokenized by a low-rank linear patch embedder (Li
& He, 2026): a p × p strided convolution maps non-overlapping RGB patches into a bottleneck of
width 128, followed by a 1 × 1 convolution to the transformer width d. Two positional signals are
used and they act at different places. Fixed two-dimensional sine-cosine embeddings (Vaswani et al.,
2017) are added to the patch tokens only, before the [CLS] and in-context tokens are concatenated.
The rotary embeddings act inside attention, on the queries and keys after QK-norm, with an identity
rotation at the non-patch positions so that the [CLS] and in-context tokens carry no spatial position.

The final layer applies RMSNORM, adaLN modulation and a zero-initialized linear projection to

Conditioning.
The timestep is embedded with sinusoidal features followed by a two-layer SILU
(Elfwing et al., 2018) MLP. The conditioning vector c passes through a linear projection and then
enters the network twice. It is added to the timestep embedding that drives the adaLN-Zero modulation
of every block, and the same projected vector is also injected as 32 in-context tokens at a fixed block,
following the design of JiT and MAR (Li & He, 2026; Li et al., 2024b). These tokens are added
on the fourth block for B/16 and the eighth for L/16. The in-context tokens are removed before the
final image projection. On the representation pass the conditioning input is the learned null e∅, and
classifier-free guidance uses a second learned null c∅.
Encoder/decoder split.
At block Lenc the [CLS] token is read out as the global embedding. At
B/16 the readout is the raw token; at L/16 it passes through a BATCHNORM (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015).
The [CLS] token is dropped at the split and never reaches the decoder, so the decoder is a function of
the patch tokens h alone. Our SCION (B/16) splits (Lenc, Ldec) = (8, 4) of 12 blocks at width 768
with 12 heads, and SCION (L/16) splits (16, 8) of 24 at width 1024 with 16 heads.
Contrastive head.
The projection head hψ is a two-hidden-layer MLP, d →2048 →2048 →256,
followed by ℓ2-normalization. It is used by LCL and by nothing else, so it is discarded after training.
Prior.
It is a residual MLP over a single vector with additive timestep conditioning. That is,
a linear layer maps the noised embedding et′ ∈Rd to width C. The timestep is encoded with
256 sinusoidal features and a two-layer SILU MLP to width C. The hidden vector then passes
through N residual blocks, each applying RMSNORM, a SWIGLU projection to the block width,
an additive linear projection of the timestep embedding, and a linear projection back to C. A final
RMSNORM, SILU and linear layer return to Rd. Every block’s output projection and the final
projection are zero-initialized, so each block is the identity and the prior predicts zero at initialization.
The prior uses additive residual connections and it is trained with the same x-prediction flow matching
parameterization as the image path, in representation space. Table 14 gives the configuration.

Qualitative results using learned representations
SCION extracts its own semantic representations and uses them to condition generation. We explore
this connection through qualitative experiments using representations produced by the model itself.
First, we generate from real-image representations to examine whether the outputs preserve the
references’ semantic content. We then interpolate between two image representations to explore how
the generated content changes along the resulting path.
All examples use SCION (L/16) at 400 epochs. Generated images use representation guidance with
a scaling factor s = 2.0 (see Equation 6). Reference images come from the ImageNet training set,
except flower buds in Figure 6 which are external photographs. Real references show the same
256 × 256 crops supplied to the encoder. This section is meant to illustrate qualitative behavior rather
than quantify reconstruction accuracy or diversity.
G.1
Generation from real-image representations
We encode each reference image and directly supply its representation to the generator, bypassing the
prior. The pairs in Figure 4 illustrate preservation of semantic content without exact reproduction
of image details. Generated images retain recognizable subjects and scene types, while pose, color,
object markings, backgrounds, and architectural details can change. For example, a cup retains its
identity as a cup while its decoration changes, and a building remains recognizable as the same type
of structure despite changes in its facade. Thus, the model’s own representations support semantically
related generation without requiring the output to reconstruct the particular reference image.
G.2
Interpolation between image representations
We next interpolate between the representations of two real images and generate from the intermediate
vectors (Figures 5 and 6). We use spherical interpolation of their directions and linear interpolation of
their magnitudes in the generator’s conditioning space, bypassing the prior. Image-sampling noise is
held fixed within each row, so changes along a path reflect changes in the conditioning representation.
In Figure 5, the paths first explore appearance and scene composition within a class, then connect
different classes, from two dog breeds to more distant concepts such as a bottle and a dog. These
cross-class paths reveal how structures from the endpoints can blend, although their intermediate
images need not depict plausible natural objects.
Figure 6 applies the same interpolation mechanism to five selected pairs of bud and open-flower
representations. Bud photographs were obtained externally and were not part of the training set.
Open-flower photographs come from the ImageNet training set and were manually matched to the
buds. Thus, the paired photographs may depict different individual flowers. These are representation
interpolations, not observed growth sequences or predictions of a particular bud’s future appearance.
The selected paths show opening-like changes in flower structure, while endpoint color and details
can drift. The six generated images include endpoint reconstructions at α = 0 and 1; these are distinct
from the real photographs.
