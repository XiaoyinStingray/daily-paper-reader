# 16-bit Precision of Convolutional Neural Networks on Microcontroller Units for 8-bit Costs 

Rui Liu 1 and Benjamin Paaßen 11Faculty of Technology, Bielefeld University preprint as provided by the authors 

Abstract 

To deploy deep neural networks on edge hardware, highly efficient inference schemes are necessary that retain high accuracy. This work presents W16A16, a high precision (16-bit), fast speed, low energy quantization method. On a widely applied microcontroller architecture Armv7E-M, our proposed approach achieves faster speed and lower energy consumption on layer- and model-level compared to alternative quantization schemes. We analyze the architecture of Armv7E-M, explain the underlying principles behind the per-formance advantages of 16-bit approaches, and evaluate the empiric quantization errors for regression and classification tasks, as well as empiric time- and energy consumption in MCU deployment. We observe ca. 10 times lower quantization errors compared to 8-bit quantization schemes while achieving similar or better inference times and energy consumptions. 

## 1 Introduction 

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have been widely applied in various domains, such as im-age recognition [He et al., 2016], speech detection [Nassif et al., 2019] and signal regression [Qiu et al., 2014]. However, executing DNNs (also called inference) often requires substan-tial computational resources and energy [Zaidi et al., 2022]. This resource demand often makes it infeasible to deploy DNNs on resource-limited platforms, such as Microcontroller Units (MCUs) and Microprocessing Units (MPUs). MCUs are the mainstream choice for the deployment in industrial and Internet of Things (IoT) devices [Novac et al., 2021]. There-fore, making DNNs executable on MCUs would unlock substantial capabilities for such edge devices. Typical MCUs have very limited memory, often less than 1 MB, and low clock speeds (often 40 to 500 Mhz) [Deutel et al., 2023]. However, industrial and IoT scenarios often demand very small delays (real-time reaction), particularly in safety-critical applications, such as Health Monitoring [Alshehri and Muhammad, 2020], industrial IoT, predictive maintenance and autonomous vehicles [Yurtsever et al., 2020]. As such, DNNs in such scenarios not only need to be small (in terms of memory) but also fast (in terms of cycles required for executing an entire DNN inference). To overcome the challenge of limited resources on edge devices, the research field of TinyML emerged, focusing on the deployment of complex DNNs models by means of novel al-gorithms, hardware-software co-designs, neural network pruning [Liberis and Lane, 2023] and quantization [Rusci et al., 2020]. Among these methods, quantization has become the most 1arXiv:2610.03402v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 preprint as provided by the authors 2widely utilized approach [Nagel et al., 2021]. Quantization refers to the rounding of 32-bit floating point numbers (which typically represents weights and activations in DNNs) to lower-resolution integers (e.g. 8-bit, 16-bit). This does not only reduce the memory footprint of DNNs but also enables the use of integer arithmetic components on the MCU, which are typ-ically much faster and more energy-efficient compared to floating point units. Consequently, 8-bit integer (INT8) quantization has been been widely used and deeply optimized by various embedded AI frameworks and libraries, such as Tensorflow-Micro-Light [David et al., 2021], X-CUBE-AI [Falbo et al., 2019], and CMSIS-NN [Lai et al., 2018]. Indeed, INT8 quantiza-tion has become a standard approach for MCUs [Abushahla et al., 2025]. However, the loss of resolution due to INT8 quantization is so severe that the accuracy of DNNs may be affected, especially for regression tasks (as we show in our experiments). As such, higher resolution via 16-bit quantization would be desirable—but may risk a higher energy- and memory footprint. Fortunately, in this paper, we show that the instruction set of the widely used ARMv7E-M architecture enables us to run INT16 quantized neural nets with almost the same time- and energy efficiency compared to INT8 quantized nets while achieving almost the same accuracy as the full FLOAT32 network. In other words: we get 16-bit resolution for the cost of 8-bit resolution. In summary, our contributions are: (1) We analyze the ARMv7E-M architecture and its assembly instruction set to derive a novel approach for INT16 quantized DNNs on MCUs. (2) We implement this approach in software for the example of temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) and provide this implementation as an open source package. (3) We empirically demonstrate superior performance of INT16 TCNs compared to INT8 TCNs in both classi-fication and regression tasks, on par with the full FLOAT32 version of the model. (4) We empirically demonstrate that the number of computations, inference time, and energy con-sumption of our proposed INT16 implementation is comparable to INT8 quantization and outperforms a mixed quantization scheme (8-bit weights, 16-bit activations) as well as refer-ence quantization schemes implemented as part of the Cube.AI library. The implementation of our approach is available at https://gitlab.com/fastconv1d/ W16A16 .

When dealing with deep neural nets (DNNs), quantization is applied to both weights and activations to speed up the execution of the DNN. Because different resolutions may be applied to weights and activations, we will adopt a notation that makes the resolution explicit for both weights and activations, namely the letter W, followed by the resolutions for the weights of a DNN, followed by the letter A, followed by the resolution for the activations of a DNN. Our proposed quantization scheme in this work is W16A16, meaning 16-bit quantization for both weights and activations. 

2.1.1 Static v.s. Dynamic Quantization 

The aforementioned quantization formula (1) is also called static quantization, which sets the quantization parameters S, Z, Qmin and Qmax statically before the DNN is executed. By contrast, it is also possible to adjust these parameters dynamically to the current activa-tions during DNN execution to better match the incoming data [Zhao et al., 2024]. However, dynamic quantization requires substantial computation overhead to select the correct param-eters, which is infeasible on MCUs. Therefore, TinyML, including our approach in this paper, employs static quantization to avoid computationally intensive floating-point operations. 

2.1.2 Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Quantization 

Quantization with Z = 0 is called symmetric, whereas quantization with Z̸ = 0 is called asymmetric [Gholami et al., 2022]. The weight distribution of convolutional neural networks preprint as provided by the authors 4is typically symmetric around zero, such that symmetric quantization is viable [Lai et al., 2018] (refer to Fig. 2). However, the distribution of activations may well be asymmetric (refer to Fig. 1), suggesting asymmetric quantization. However, asymmetric quantization comes at a price: a convolutional layer involves thousands of weight-activation multiplications of the form 

w1 × x1. If the activations are quantized asymmetrically, these operations always require the form w1 ×(x1 −Z), meaning Z has to be subtracted before the multiplication can be executed. This leads to an increase in execution cycles and register usage. For 8-bit quantization, this overhead unavoidable without major quantization errors. However, for our proposed 16-bit quantization, the value range is so much larger (-32768 to +32767 instead of -128 to 127) that the entire activation range can fit inside without major errors (as we will show in the experiments). Hence, our proposed W16A16 scheme will use symmetric quantization for both weights and activations. 

2.1.3 Per-layer vs. per-channel quantization 

Quantization can also be distinguished into per-layer and per-channel [Abushahla et al., 2025]. Per-layer quantization employs the same quantization parameters for each floating point num-ber in an entire layer of a DNN, whereas per-channel quantization uses different parameters per output of weights, thus accounting for inter-channel variation. In practice, it is a stan-dard method for convolutional neural network due to channel-wise variation [Abushahla et al., 2025]. Accordingly, this paper will employ per-layer for activations and per-channel quanti-zation for weights.

2.2.2 Fixed-point approximation of float scaling 

The scaling factor Srequantization,c is actually a floating-point number, which would make Equation (2) a floating point multiplication. To avoid such a float operation, we wish to approximate the multiplication in Equation (2) with integer operations. In the early version of CMSIS-NN [Lai et al., 2018], a Q-format fixed-point quantization (e.g. Q15/Q7) is performed to approximate the scaling factor multiplication, e.g. via the 

arm_convolve_HWC_q15 function. This scheme approximates the multiplication with S by an n-bit shift. Accordingly, only powers of two are available as scaling factors. However, actual scaling factors are rarely exact powers of two, resulting in large quantization errors. For example, a scaling factor of 0.1 would have to be approximated by the nearest power of 2, namely 2−3 = 0 .125 , resulting in an error of 25%. Therefore, more accurate approximation schemes have been developed, which instead de-compose the scaling factor S as S ≈ MQ31 × 2−31 −n, where MQ31 is a 32-bit integer, such that preprint as provided by the authors 6more values in the range [0 , 1] can be represented. [Jacob et al., 2018]. For example, a mul-tiplication with S = 0 .1 can be approximated as first multiplying with MQ31 = 1717986918

2.3 The Cortex-M processor instruction set 

2.3.1 ARMv7E-M Dual MAC operations 

The Cortex-M processor applies sub-word parallelism to maximize throughput. According to [Lai et al., 2018], the ARMv7E-M architecture has the capability to divide each 32-bit register into two 16-bit sub-words which enables dual multiplication and accumulation (MAC) instruc-tions (e.g. SMLAD and SMLALD). SMLAD executes double INT16 x INT16 MAC within a single clock cycle. However, SMLAD doesn’t support four INT8 x INT8 MAC operations. Accordingly, INT8 and INT16 MAC operations are similarly fast on the ARMv7E-M. Indeed, INT8 operations introduce some overhead because operands first need to be unpacked and preprocessed before SMLAD instructions can be executed, whereas INT16 operands have the correct width right away. Importantly, most operations involved in executing a convolutional neural network (or any DNN) are MAC operations. Thus, INT8 and INT16 quantized DNNs are similarly fast. Taking the INT8 convolution layer operator arm_convolve_s8 from the CMSIS-NN li-brary as an example, sign extension instruction and re-ordering instruction are required for data preprocessing. In the arm_convolve_s8 function, the Sign extend two Bytes with Add 

instruction (SXTAB16) extends 8-bit weight data into a 16-bit format and performs a Ze-roPoint addition for asymmetric quantization. Finally, the re-ordering instructions PKHTB and PKHBT are used to ensure the correct order of the output data. By contrast, loading INT16 data does not require such data pre- or postprocessing instruc-tions. This results in a speed advantage of INT16 compared to INT8 processing. The detailed comparison of INT8 and INT16 precessing on the instruction level is provided in Section 3.2. 

2.3.2 Single-issue vs. Dual-issue 

The M4 and M7 Micro-Architecture utilize the same ARMv7E-M DSP Instruction Set. How-ever, they differ in the execution flows. The Cortex-M4 processor allows only a single instruc-tion to be executed per clock cycle following the in-order strictly. By contrast, Cortex-M7 employs a 6-stage superscalar pipeline architecture with dual-issue capability [Lorenser, 2016]. The Dual-issue capability allows the processor to execute two in-structions within the same clock cycle, thus increasing inference speed. However, Instruction-level parallelism (ILP) has strict requirements: The dual-issue pair must not have data depen-dencies such as Read After Write (RAW). Our paper evaluates different quantization methods with both the M4 and the M7 Micro-Architecture to ensure the advantages of W16A16 quan-tization method across two architectures.

3.3 Memory Layout and Convolutional operator design 

Before discussing the design and implementation of convolutional operators on MCUs in detail, it is necessary to first explain the memory layout of input and weight data. Fig. 7 shows an example of the implementation of a 1D convolutional layer. The input data has a shape of (T, IC ), where T is the number of time steps and IC the number of input channels, and each entry in the memory represents the data of one input channel at one time step. In this example, the number of input channels is three, meaning each time step T contains three elements IN0, IN1, and IN2. The shape of the weight tensor is (OC, K, IC ), where OC denotes the number of output channels and K denotes the size of kernel. As Fig. 7 illustrates, the lowest-level data in memory consists of input channels, grouped into packages for each kernel window (K0, K1, K2), which in turn are grouped into packages for each output channel (OC0, OC1, ...). Accordingly, the memory layouts of inputs and weights do not match. When executing the convolution operation, the 1DCNN’s receptive field (with kernel size K = 3) needs to slide as a window across the input data. As Fig. 7 shows, after the CPU finishes processing all elements within the receptive field of the first Window0 at Step 8, it must jump back to the location to Step 9, which is the start point of the next window. This jump operation is a discontinuous memory access, resulting in longer access times. For 2D convolution operations, the Im2col (image to column) method is proposed to address this problem by flattening and rearranging the input data within a receptive field [Chellapilla et al., 2006]. This method transforms the complicated multi-channels sliding window into a one-dimensional General Matrix Multiplication (GEMM). For our time series data, we also need a transformation like Im2col. For the 1D case, we name the transformation Seq2col (sequence to column). Fig. 8 demonstrates the principle of Seq2Col. The sliding window process is decomposed and flattened into a series of windows, where L denotes the number of windows. After the processing of Seq2col, the input data shape is converted into the shape (L, IC, K ),while the shape of the weights is (OC, IC, K ). As Fig. 8 shows, the windows are put into sequential order after Seq2col, thereby simplifying memory access. In Fig. 9, we use a causal convolutional layer with kernel size K = 3 , dilation d = 2 , and 3 input channels as example. First, causal left padding of size (K − 1) × d is applied to ensure the causal relationship of data. Second, the input channels (e.g. IN0, IN1, IN2) are padded to an even number (e.g. 3 to 4) to ensure memory addresses are aligned to multiples of four. Thereby, dual-word load instructions such as LDRD can be utilized to accelerate data loading; this instruction requires the address to be aligned to a multiple of 4 and its execution speed is twice that of LDR. Third, following the padding phase, the receptive field of a causal dilated convolutional layer is constructed by fetching the discontinuous dilated input data from original input data, resulting in the first time step [0 , 0, X 0] (illustrated in green in Fig. 9), and the second time step [0 , 0, X 1] (illustrated in blue in Fig. 9). Finally, these data blocks are concatenated into a one-dimensional continuous linear array, enabling CPU to execute LDRD and SMLAD instructions on the data structure. Additionally, the 2D input array of shape (IC, K ) is flattened into shape (IC · K, 1) to prepare for the Activation-Weight-Multiplication in next step. We break down the multiplication of the (L, IC, K ) activation tensor and the (OC, IC, K )

weight tensor into three nested for-loops: the outermost loop iterates over L, the second over OC, and the innermost over the dot product between input (IC ×K) and weight (IC ×K).The memory access of input data and weight data is continuous and can be accelerated with the dual-MAC instructions like SMLAD or SMLALD.

As previous explained, Multiplier is an integer number (1 sign bit + 31 data bits), and shifting is normally above 30. The combination of these two operations approximates the multiplication with a floating-point scaling factor (e.g. 0.1). To construct a larger example, suppose that after the Accumulator ×M ultiplier operation, the intermediate result is stored within three 32-bit registers (so 96 bits in total). The valid data of the intermediate result spans bits 0 through 45, while bits 46 through 95 are sign-extension. Assume we need to shift by 34 bits (e.g. to represent the multipliers 0.1). Then, the valid data stored at bits 34 through 45 is safely shifted down to bits 0 through 11. The discarded lower 34 bits of data represent actually the fractional part of the floating-point approximation. The data in bits 0 through 11 are the valid value required for the input of next layer. Depending on the number of remaining bits after shifting, we designed three different processing schemes: 

No saturation required (normal range): If the data after shifting Result shif ted falls within 16 bits (1 sign-bit and 15 data-bit) range, 

−215 ≤ xshif ted ≤ 215 − 1

, we use the data in low-order register directly as the input of the next convolutional layer.

−231 ≤ xshif ted < −215 or 215 − 1 < x shif ted ≤ 231 − 1preprint as provided by the authors 16 , we utilize the assembly instruction SSAT. y16 = min(max( Result shif ted , −215 ), 215 − 1) 

Two-Stage Saturation (extreme overflow): In extreme cases, the data after shifting may exceed the representation limit of 32-bit, i.e. 

xshif ted < −231 or xshif ted > 231 − 1.

If we were to directly process bits 0 through 31 bits, the sign-bit in the higher registers would be ignored, yielding extreme quantization errors. For example, 231 (0x80000000) is a positive number that exceeds the maximum representable range ( 231 − 1) of a 32-bit signed register. Hence, it is falsely interpreted as −1 in the 32-bit signed register. To avoid this mistake, we should first truncate 231 to 231 − 1, which is the maximum value for a 32-bit signed register. Accordingly, we propose a two stage Saturation scheme: we first saturate the data to the representation limit of 32-bit 

y32 = min(max( xshif ted , −231 ), 231 − 1) 

, then saturate it to 16-bit using the assembly instruction SSAT. 

y16 = min(max( y32 , −215 ), 215 − 1) 

.After this process, we obtain the result for next convolutional layer and avoid the overflow risk. Importantly, note that the saturation operations can implement a rectified linear unit (ReLU) transition function for a convolutional neural network by emplyoing the USAT oper-ation instead of the SSAT operation. 

## 4 Evaluation results 

In our experiments, we compare three quantization approaches, W8A8, W8A16, and W16A16 (our proposed scheme) in terms of performance in machine learning tasks as well as execution time and energy consumption. The implementation of our experiment is available at: https: //gitlab.com/fastconv1d/tcn_experiments 

4.1 Hardware and Configuration 

To comprehensively evaluate the latency, energy consumption and cross-platform versatility of our proposed quantization methods, we deployed our model and baselines on two represen-tative MCUs. • STM32H723ZG: ARM Cortex-M7 core with a maximum frequency of 550MHz, inte-grating with I-Cache and D-Cache with FPU. • GD32F450VET: ARM Cortex-M4 core with a maximum frequency of 200MHz, sup-ported by FPU. Both MCUs support the ARMv7E-M architecture and DSP extension instruction sets. preprint as provided by the authors 17 

Quantization settings All machine learning models were trained using floating point num-bers and then quantized after training (a so-called post training quantization). The W8A8 quantization was implemented using the official Pytorch.tq toolbox. However, this toolbox does not support W8A16 or W16A16 quantization. Consequently, we developed custom quantization approaches. During the calibration phase, we scaled the maximum observed ac-tivation values by a factor of 2.0 to prevent the precision loss due to truncation of unexpected activation outliers. 

Compiler settings All C/Assembly inference operators are compiled using Arm Compiler 6, with -Ofast and link-time optimization (-flto). To measure the inference cycles precisely, we insert memory barrier instructions to eliminate the influence of global optimization on the measurement results.

4.2.1 C-MAPSS Dataset 

The C-MAPSS FD001 sub-dataset is provided by NASA, which is a standard benchmark for Remaining Useful Life (RUL) estimation of engines [Asif et al., 2022]. During flight, it is impossible to upload massive amounts of time-series data to the cloud, such that process-ing needs to occur at the engine itself using an MCU. We use this dataset to evaluate the quantization errors of different quantization approaches. The dataset consists of 100 train engines and 100 test engines with 26 input channels. To prevent early-life overestimation when the engine still works in a healthy state, we set a upper-bound threshold of 125 flights. The input channels contains 12 uninformative features, therefore, we preserve 14 input channels for our regression task [Asif et al., 2022]. The window size of input data is set as 30 time steps [Yu et al., 2021]. We utilized a TCN model as shown in Fig. 3 to perform the regression task. The TCN consists of 4 stacked TCN Blocks with each convolutional layer followed by weight normaliza-tion and each ReLU followed by dropout (dropout rate 0.3). The channel size of each block is 32, with kernel size of 5 and dilation 2i where i is the index of the TCN block (higher dilation for higher layers). The final layer is a 1 convolutional layer to produce the actual RUL prediction for the engine. We trained the TCN for 50 epochs with an Adam optimizer and the mean square error loss function, a batch size of 64, and a learning rate of 0.001. The RMSE of the resulting non-quantized model against the ground truth is 13.1977 which suggests reasonable model fit [Wang et al., 2018]. 4.2.2 NinaPro DB2 force regression 

We utilize NinaPro DB2 as a second regression evaluation dataset [Gijsberts et al., 2014]. The task is to map surface electromyographic (sEMG) signals recorded at the lower arm of a person to the desired activation force of a hand prosthesis. This data set is particularly suitable for MCUs as the control of an arm prosthesis needs to be performed on the prosthesis itself using as little power and computation as possible [Islam et al., 2026]. Additionally, the force estimation via the regression model is very sensitive to the quantization noise. Therefore, this task serves as a useful benchmark for the evaluation of quantization approaches. The NinaPro DB2 dataset contains data from 40 subjects, collecting sEMG signals via 12 channels at a sampling frequency at 2000Hz; the dataset includes 6 force signal channels, serving as targets for the regression task. The window size of our model is set to 300 samples; the window increment is set to 20 for the training set and 10 samples for the test set. Using finer increments during testing better simulates high-frequency mechanical control in the prosthesis. Our regression model for NinaPro DB2 is similar to the model described in Sec. 4.2.1: it is also a TCN model composed of four TCN blocks and employs the same hyperparam-eters mentioned above, except that the input has 12 input channels and 6 output channels (corresponding to 12 sEMG channels and 6 force channels). preprint as provided by the authors 19 We use median value across 40 subjects to represent the RMSE of various quantization approaches, thereby avoiding the impact of outliers. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose a W16A16 quantization scheme to execute deep neural networks, in particular time convolutional neural networks on MCUs. We provided the exact assembly instructions needed to run W16A16 quantization efficiently, implemented the quantization scheme as an open source package, and performed experiments to compare W16A16 quanti-zation to W8A8 and W8A16 quantization across two hardware platforms and three machine learning tasks. Our results challenge the notion that lower quantization precision automati-cally leads to faster speed. Our W16A16 quantization method is both faster (ca. 17.6% for a full TCN model on STM32H7) and more energy-efficient than W8A16 across MCUs, at both layer-wise and end-to-end model level, while providing higher accuracy in both regression and classification tasks. While W8A8 is slightly faster and more energy efficient compared to our proposed W16A16 scheme, the empiric classification and regression performance of the quantized neural net is significantly worse (ca. 100x quantization error in regression tasks). Therefore, W16A16 provides an attractive option for applications with high accuracy require-ments under tight energy and time constraints. This work also provides the explanation for the empiric observations based on the underly-ing architecture: in the Cortex-M instruction set, the maximum throughput for integer MAC operations is the same for 8-bit and 16-bit operations and 8-bit operations require additional pre- and post-processing instructions, thus yielding overhead for W8A16 quantization. Based on our architecture analysis, we modified the 64-bit requantization provided by CMSIS-NN, and designed a Full-Precision 96-bit Cross-Register Shift and Two-Stage saturation to avoid the overflow of intermediate values. Further work could explore whether such architecture-specific resolution gains with little energy/time overhead can also be translated to other MCUs or hardware architectures. 

5.1 Limitations 

The cost advantage of our proposed W16A16 quantization method is based on the architecture of ARMv7E-M which exhibits the same speed for 8-bit and 16-bit operations. However, this special effect does not necessarily translate to newer architectures such as ARMv8.1-M or RISC-V, which are capable to process four 8-bit data elements in a single instruction of MAC operation. Still, ARMv7E-M is a widely applied cortex-M architecture, providing a wide scope of application of our approach at this time. We also note that our experiments are limited to TCN models and three machine learning tasks across two data sets. On other data sets, other tasks, and other models, results may be slightly different. Still, given the consistent patterns we observed across metrics, data sets, and hardware in our experiments, we are fairly confident that the qualitative pattern would generalize: W16A16 is faster than W8A16 and roughly comparable to W8A8, while W8A8 is significantly less precise compared to the other two.
